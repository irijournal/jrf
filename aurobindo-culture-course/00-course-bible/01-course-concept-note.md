## Summary

This seminar reads three bodies of work by Sri Aurobindo closely, in their historical setting and in conversation with their critics: the four essays of The Renaissance in India (Arya, 1918), the essays published as 'Is India Civilised?' and A Defence of Indian Culture (Arya, 1918 to 1921), and the five complete verse plays written between about 1905 and 1915. Each participant carries one question of their own from Day 1 to a capstone essay defended before a panel on Day 30, and each takes part in a staged reading of a scene from the plays.

The course has a single governing aim: to teach participants to understand an argument fully before judging it, and then to judge it fairly. It neither asks participants to accept Sri Aurobindo's conclusions nor permits them to dismiss him by label. They must reconstruct his arguments, steelman his critics, test his claims against evidence, and say where the arguments hold and where they do not.

## 1. Why these texts

The Renaissance in India and the Defence are among the most ambitious accounts of Indian culture written in the colonial period. They answer a real controversy, the attack on Indian civilisation in William Archer's India and the Future (1917), and they widen into a theory of how a culture lives, declines, meets other cultures and renews itself. Their central ideas (spirit and form, renaissance against revival, assimilation against imitation) remain useful in debates about culture, tradition and modernity.

The plays are rarely taught. They show the same mind working in another form: deliverance from a cruel order in Perseus the Deliverer, love and statecraft in Vasavadutta, a tragedy of rivalry in Rodogune, just rule and good counsel in The Viziers of Bassora, and the transformation of enmity in Eric. Read beside the essays, they turn abstract claims into human action, and they can be performed.

## 2. The educational problem

Courses on Indian thought often fall into one of two failures. Reverent courses paraphrase and admire; participants learn what the author believed but not how to test it. Critical courses apply a theory (postcolonial, Marxist, feminist) before the text has been understood; participants learn to label rather than to read. Neither teaches judgement.

The seminar answers both failures with a single method, used every day: the Reader's Ledger, which keeps four things apart: what the text says (with a reference), what it implies, what the reader judges, and what the reader still needs to ask. Around it sit a small set of argument tools (reconstruction, claim, ground and warrant, steelmanning, standards analysis) and a small set of literary tools (source comparison, structure, character arc, verse, staging).

## 3. Audience and prior knowledge

Participants need to read sustained English prose and write clear paragraphs. No knowledge of Sanskrit, Indian philosophy or drama is assumed. Each day's context section, the Text Guide and the Model Readings supply what is needed. Participants from Indian knowledge systems programmes will find the course rigorous about evidence; participants from literature and history programmes will find it serious about Indian concepts.

## 4. Principles

1. **Understand before judging.** Every judgement must follow an accurate reconstruction of the text.
2. **Text, inference, judgement, question.** Keep them apart, every day, in the Reader's Ledger.
3. **Steelman the critics.** Archer, Ambedkar, Said and others are read at their strongest.
4. **Every claim needs a standard.** Name the standard by which a culture, a work of art or an argument is judged.
5. **The plays are read aloud.** Drama is heard and staged, not only analysed.
6. **One question, carried all the way.** Each participant's question develops from Day 1 to the capstone.
7. **Accuracy is a moral matter.** No invented quotation, page number or fact; AI output is never evidence of what the author wrote.

## 5. Architecture

| Week | Theme | Days | Gate |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Foundations: the life, the Arya, the colonial debate, key concepts | 1 to 5 | Gate 1: reading journal and capstone question |
| 2 | The Renaissance in India and 'Indian Culture and External Influence' | 6 to 10 | Gate 2: short essay, 1,200 words |
| 3 | The Defence: 'Is India Civilised?', Archer, religion, the aims of life, the social ideal | 11 to 15 | Gate 3: argument map and response |
| 4 | The Defence: art, literature, polity | 16 to 20 | Gate 4: comparative essay, 1,500 words |
| 5 | Judgement of the Defence; the dramatist; Perseus, Vasavadutta, Rodogune | 21 to 25 | Gate 5: scene analysis, 1,200 words |
| 6 | The Viziers of Bassora, Eric, performance studio, capstone | 26 to 30 | Capstone: essay, presentation, defence, staged reading |

Each teaching day has: a central question; one anchor method; a passage of the day for close reading; a voice 'in conversation' with the text (from Arnold, Macaulay and Burckhardt to Ambedkar, Said, Ramanujan, Bhasa and Corneille); one group activity; and one worksheet that links the day's reading to the participant's own question.

## 6. Teaching model

The default two-hour session: opening question (10 minutes); concept and context (25); close reading of the passage of the day (20); group activity (35); the Reader's Ledger worksheet (20); debrief and exit task (10). Gate days shorten the concept block to allow 40 minutes of peer review. Play days replace close reading with a read-through of the scene of the day. Days 28 to 30 are studio and capstone days.

## 8. Accuracy and sources

All primary texts are cited from The Complete Works of Sri Aurobindo (CWSA), published by the Sri Aurobindo Ashram, Pondicherry: Volume 20 for the essays and Volumes 3 and 4 for the plays. The course materials paraphrase rather than quote wherever possible. Where a well-known sentence is quoted, the materials ask the instructor to check its wording against the printed text. Details of the plays that could not be verified when these materials were written (for example the setting and the names in Perseus the Deliverer) are marked for checking in the instructor notes. Instructors must check every quotation they put on a slide or handout.

## 9. Distinctive features

1. **Arguments and plays together.** The essays argue; the plays enact. The course reads both and asks how they illuminate each other.
2. **The controversy restored.** Archer and Woodroffe are read, not just mentioned, so that the Defence is understood as a reply.
3. **Critics at full strength.** Ambedkar on caste, Said on Orientalism and Ramanujan on plural epics are read alongside the texts.
4. **Performance.** Every participant speaks verse aloud and takes part in a staged reading.
5. **One's own question.** The capstone is the participant's own argument, developed over six weeks.
6. **A regional anchor.** Sanchi, Khajuraho, Gwalior and Ujjain (the setting of Vasavadutta) give participants in Madhya Pradesh direct access to works the essays discuss.

## 10. Risks and how they are handled

| Risk | Mitigation |
|---|---|
| Reverence or hostility replaces reading | The Reader's Ledger; steelmanning; rubrics that reward reconstruction before judgement |
| Painful subjects (caste) become personal | Norms read aloud on Day 15; argument with texts, not people |
| Inaccurate quotation and invented facts | Paraphrase by default; quotations checked; AI-use policy |
| Unfamiliarity with verse | Read-throughs every play day; no technical vocabulary required |
| Overload | One anchor method a day; essential reading is always the primary text |
| Partisan use of the course | The course is not affiliated with any political or religious body; it teaches judgement, not doctrine |
