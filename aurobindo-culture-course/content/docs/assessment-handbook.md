# Spirit and Form
## Assessment Handbook

This handbook sets out how participants are assessed: the components and weights, the five gates, the rubrics, the feedback format, the AI-use policy, the entry and exit diagnostic, and the completion standard.

## 1. Principles

1. **Assess reading through writing and speaking.** Every mark attaches to something a participant produced.
2. **Understand before judging.** Rubrics reward accurate reconstruction before they reward judgement.
3. **Reward revision.** Gates are formative; the portfolio is marked once at the end, with credit for documented revision.
4. **One set of criteria.** Every rubric uses the same four dimensions, so feedback in Week 1 is useful in Week 6.
5. **Individual work is individual.** Seminar and company work carry a minority of marks.

## 2. Components and weights

| Component | Weight | What is assessed | When |
|---|---|---|---|
| Reading journal and worksheets | 25% | The Reader's Ledger and 30 worksheets, marked as a portfolio with credit for revision | Gates 1 to 5; portfolio after Day 30 |
| Gate essays and maps | 25% | Gate 2 essay (6%), Gate 3 map and response (6%), Gate 4 essay (7%), Gate 5 scene analysis (6%) | Days 10, 15, 20, 25; marked at the end |
| Seminar contribution and peer review | 10% | Quality of written peer reviews and of contribution to activities | Throughout |
| Staged reading | 10% | Company interpretation (5%) and individual verse speaking (5%) | Day 30 |
| Capstone essay | 20% | 3,000 to 4,000 words, revised after the defence | 72 hours after Day 30 |
| Presentation and defence | 10% | Six-minute presentation and six-minute defence | Day 30 |

Gate 1 (the Week 1 reading journal and capstone question) receives feedback but no separate mark; it is part of the portfolio.

## 3. The common rubric

Every gate, the capstone and the defence use four dimensions on three levels.

| Dimension | Foundational | Working | Advanced |
|---|---|---|---|
| Understanding | Summarises with errors or gaps | Reconstructs arguments or scenes accurately, with references | Reconstructs subtly, noticing qualifications and development across texts |
| Analysis | Describes; few distinctions | Separates text, inference and judgement; distinguishes evidence from illustration | Weighs competing readings; surfaces hidden standards and assumptions |
| Judgement | Reacts: praise or dismissal | Judges with reasons; states critics fairly | Concedes where necessary and still argues; original and well-defended |
| Communication | Unclear structure; quotations unchecked or unreferenced | Clear structure; accurate references | Elegant and economical; every quotation earns its place |

## 4. The gates

| Gate | Day | Submission | Rubric emphasis | Feedback returned |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 5 | Week 1 reading journal; concept cards; capstone question | Understanding; accuracy | By Day 7 |
| 2 | 10 | Essay, 1,200 words, reconstructing and assessing one argument from The Renaissance in India | Understanding; analysis | By Day 12 |
| 3 | 15 | Argument map and 300-word response on a Week 3 debate | Analysis; judgement | By Day 17 |
| 4 | 20 | Comparative essay, 1,500 words, on art, literature or polity in the Defence | Analysis; judgement | By Day 22 |
| 5 | 25 | Close analysis, 1,200 words, of one scene from Perseus, Vasavadutta or Rodogune | Analysis; communication | By Day 27 |

Every gate submission includes a revision note (what changed since peer review, and why) and an AI-use disclosure.

## 5. Feedback format

All gate feedback uses five headings, so that participants can act on it.

| Heading | What it gives |
|---|---|
| Strongest element | What to keep and build on |
| Critical weakness | The one thing most limiting the work |
| Missing dimension | What the work does not yet do (for example, a critic not addressed) |
| Required revision | A specific change, located in a paragraph |
| Next reading task | What to read or reread next |

Recurring patterns are logged in each participant's progress tracker, for example: paraphrase instead of reconstruction; judgement without reasons; quotations without references; reading the later philosophy back into the plays.

## 6. AI-use policy

- Participants may use AI tools to test the structure of an argument, suggest counter-arguments, or improve prose.
- Participants may not use AI output as evidence of what Sri Aurobindo, or any other author, wrote. AI tools invent quotations, page numbers and plot details.
- Every quotation must be checked against the printed text and referenced by essay or act and scene, and page.
- Every submission carries a disclosure: which tools were used, for what, and what was checked.
- Undisclosed use, or an invented quotation, is treated as a breach of academic integrity.

## 7. Entry and exit diagnostic

Before Day 1, each participant reads a short passage (about 400 words) from one of the Renaissance essays and answers three questions in 45 minutes: what does the passage argue; what does it assume; is the argument sound, and why. In the final week, the same task is set with a parallel passage. Scripts are anonymised and mixed before scoring against the common rubric. The change in the cohort's scores is the programme's first measure of whether it works.

## 8. Completion and distinction

Three options for the host institution to choose from:

| Option | Completion | Distinction |
|---|---|---|
| A (recommended) | 80% attendance; all gates submitted; capstone completed; Working level in every domain | Advanced level in at least four of the eight domains, including judgement |
| B | 80% attendance; overall mark of 50% | Overall mark of 70% |
| C | Attendance and submission only (non-credit settings) | Not awarded |

## 9. Moderation and integrity

A second reader blind-marks a sample of capstone essays (at least one in five, and every essay near a boundary). Disagreements of more than one level on any dimension are discussed and resolved. Plagiarism, invented quotations and undisclosed AI use are referred under the host institution's academic integrity procedures.
