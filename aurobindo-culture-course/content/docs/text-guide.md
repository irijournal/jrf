# Spirit and Form
## Text Guide: the essays, the plays and the key terms

This guide introduces each text read in the seminar: when and why it was written, what it argues or dramatises, what to look for, and questions to bring to class. It paraphrases rather than quotes. Check every quotation you use against the printed text in The Complete Works of Sri Aurobindo (CWSA), Volume 20 for the essays and Volumes 3 and 4 for the plays, and cite essay or act and scene as well as page.

## 1. Chronology

| Year | Event |
|---|---|
| 1872 | Born in Calcutta on 15 August |
| 1879 to 1893 | Childhood and education in England; St Paul's School, London; King's College, Cambridge |
| 1893 to 1906 | Service in Baroda State; learns Sanskrit and Indian languages; early political essays |
| 1905 | Writes The Viziers of Bassora in Baroda |
| 1906 to 1908 | Nationalist leader in Bengal; the Bande Mataram |
| 1907 | Perseus the Deliverer published serially in the weekly Bande Mataram |
| 1908 to 1909 | Arrested in the Alipore Bomb Case (May 1908); a year in jail; acquitted (1909) |
| 1910 | Leaves British India; settles in Pondicherry |
| 1914 to 1921 | The Arya, a monthly philosophical review |
| About 1915 | Vasavadutta, the last of the five complete plays |
| 1918 | 'The Renaissance in India' (August to November); 'Is India Civilised?' begins (December) |

| 1953 | Many of the culture essays published in New York under a title not his own, The Foundations of Indian Culture |

Check dates against a biography and the CWSA Notes on the Texts before teaching.

## 2. The Renaissance in India (Arya, August to November 1918)

**Occasion.** Four essays on whether India was undergoing a renaissance, a question then in the air; the Irish poet James H. Cousins had published a book called The Renaissance in India in 1918. Revised for a booklet in 1920.

**What it argues, in outline.**
- The word 'renaissance' fits India only if redefined: India's culture did not die, so its renaissance is a re-awakening and a rebirth of an old spirit in new forms, not the recovery of a dead past.
- India's past was marked by three powers together: a spirituality he calls the master-key of the Indian mind, an abundant vitality and a strong intellectuality.
- A decline set in before the modern period; the impact of Europe acted as a shock that roused the culture.
- The modern response moved through stages: absorbing European influences, reacting by asserting Indian forms, and beginning a creative synthesis.
- A true renaissance has three tasks: to recover the old spiritual knowledge in its fullness; to pour it into new forms of philosophy, literature, art, science and critical knowledge; and to deal originally with modern problems in the light of the Indian spirit, aiming at a spiritualised society.

**What to look for.** How he redefines his key word; what evidence he gives for the three powers; whether the stages describe history or prescribe a programme; the difference between renaissance and revival.

**Questions to bring.** Is 'continuity of spirit' a historical claim or a philosophical one? Does the stress on vitality and intellect qualify the master-key claim? Which of the three tasks has India done best since 1918?

## 3. 'Indian Culture and External Influence'

**What it argues, in outline.** A living culture cannot avoid meeting foreign influences. To be overwhelmed by them is death; to shut them out is also a kind of death. The right course is to take in what is useful and transform it in one's own spirit.

**What to look for.** The criteria for telling assimilation from imitation; his examples; the assumption that a culture has a centre strong enough to assimilate.

**Questions to bring.** Is the Indian Constitution assimilation or imitation? Does Tagore's openness differ from his?

## 4. 'Is India Civilised?' (Arya, December 1918 to February 1919)

**Occasion.** Begins from Sir John Woodroffe's Is India Civilized? Essays on Indian Culture (Madras: Ganesh, 1918), itself a reply to William Archer's India and the Future (London: Hutchinson, 1917).

**What it argues, in outline.** Whether India is civilised depends on what civilisation means. He argues against a merely defensive apologetics and for a living, creative response that would let India's culture survive and grow in the modern world.

**What to look for.** The standard of judgement he proposes; the warning against defending everything; the turn from the past to the future.

## 5. A Defence of Indian Culture (Arya, February 1919 to January 1921)

**Occasion.** A detailed reply to Archer, treated as representative of a type of Western criticism. The Defence is unfinished: he later said he had meant to revise it and omit all but brief references to Archer.

**The main sections, in outline.**

| Section | What it argues |
|---|---|
| The reply to 'a rationalistic critic' | Archer judges by a narrow rationalist standard and with too little knowledge; India's decline is real but not its essence. |
| Religion and spirituality | Indian religion at its best is plastic and many-sided, allowing many paths to a spiritual aim; religiosity without spirit is a degeneration. |
| Spirituality and life | Against the charge of world-negation: the four aims (dharma, artha, kama, moksha) and the stages of life give life a full place. |
| The social order | An early ideal of social types and functions differs from the rigid hereditary caste of later centuries, which he treats as degeneration. |
| Art | Indian architecture, sculpture and painting aim at spiritual expression, not imitation of nature; periods of greatness and of decline must be distinguished. |
| Literature | The Veda and Upanishads are supreme poetry as well as scripture; the epics shaped the people's mind; Kalidasa and the classical poets, and the literatures of the languages, continue the tradition. |
| Polity | Indian polity was not mere despotism: kingship under dharma, councils and assemblies, and communal self-government by villages, guilds and communities. |

**What to look for.** His method of reply (critique of the critic, concession, counter-claim, reframing); what he concedes; where he relies on illustration rather than evidence; where he describes and where he idealises.

**Critics to read beside it.** B. R. Ambedkar on caste; Max Weber on Indian religion and economic life; Edward Said on Orientalism; Partha Chatterjee on nationalist thought; Amartya Sen on the argumentative tradition; A. K. Ramanujan on plural epics.

## 6. The plays

The CWSA editors date the plays from about 1905 to 1915. Only Perseus the Deliverer was published in his lifetime; the others appeared between 1957 and 1960. CWSA Volume 3 contains The Viziers of Bassora, Rodogune and Perseus the Deliverer; Volume 4 contains Eric and Vasavadutta, with incomplete plays and stories. All five are in blank verse, drawing on Shakespearean romance, Greek drama and Kalidasa.

### The Viziers of Bassora

**Kind and date.** Romantic comedy; written in Baroda, 1905; the manuscript was seized at his arrest in 1908.

**Source.** The tale of Nur al-Din Ali and Anis al-Jalis in the Thousand and One Nights.

**People.** The caliph Haroun Alrasheed and his minister Jaafar; Alfazzal Ibn Sawy, the chief vizier of Bassora; his son Nureddene; Almuene Bin Khakan, the second vizier; Anice-aljalice, a Persian slave-girl.

**Themes.** Good and malicious counsel; reversals of fortune; the just ruler's judgement.

**Questions.** What makes the caliph's judgement just? How does the comedy relate to the essays on polity?

### Rodogune

**Kind.** Tragedy; set in Syria. The manuscript was among papers seized in 1908; published posthumously.

**Source.** The history of Cleopatra Thea, queen of Syria, told by Appian, Justin and Josephus; Pierre Corneille's tragedy Rodogune (1644 to 1645) treats the same subject.

**People.** Queen Cleopatra; her twin sons Antiochus and Timocles; Rodogune, a captive Parthian princess made an attendant of the queen, who loves Antiochus; the chancellor Phayllus.

**Themes.** Rivalry, love and a mother's power; tragedy arising from fear and ambition. Critics note that he removes much of the horror of Corneille's version and centres inner conflict.

**Questions.** Where is the tragedy: in the situation or in the people? What is the decisive choice?

### Perseus the Deliverer

**Kind and date.** Heroic romance on a Greek legend; 1906 to 1907; published serially in the weekly Bande Mataram in 1907.

**Story.** Perseus rescues Andromeda, who is threatened with sacrifice to a sea monster. In his version, the conflict is between a cruel cult and its priesthood and a new, humane order, with the goddess Athene guiding the hero. **Check the setting and the names of the king, queen and priest in CWSA 3 before teaching.**

**Themes.** Deliverance from fear and from a cruel religious order; the hero as instrument of a larger will.

**Questions.** Is the play a political allegory of India in 1907? Does it attack religion, or religiosity in the sense of the Defence?

### Eric

**Kind and date.** Romance; written in Pondicherry; first published in the Sri Aurobindo Mandir Annual, 1960.

**Source.** No known specific source; names and events from Norwegian history of the late tenth and early eleventh centuries used freely.

**People.** Eric; Swegn, his rival; Aslaug, Swegn's sister; Hertha; Hardicnut, Ragnar, Harald and Gunthar. In the last act Swegn is spared on condition of submission.

**Themes.** Enmity transformed by love; kingship and unity; love read by commentators as turning into divine love.

**Questions.** Where is Aslaug's moment of choice? What kind of king does Eric become?

### Vasavadutta

**Kind and date.** Romance; written in Pondicherry, the last of the five; published in 1957.

**Source.** By his own note, the Kathasaritsagara, Somadeva's eleventh-century Sanskrit story-cycle. Bhasa's Svapnavasavadattam (published in 1912 after its rediscovery) treats a later episode of the same story.

**People.** Vuthsa Udayan, king of Cowsambie (Kaushambi); Chunda Mahasegn, king of Avunthie (Avanti, whose capital was Ujjayini, today's Ujjain); his daughter Vasavadutta.

**Themes.** Love born through music across enmity; love and statecraft; the heroine's choice.

**Questions.** What does the play change from its source, and why? Is it a renaissance in his own sense: an old story in a new form?

### Incomplete plays

CWSA Volume 4 also contains incomplete dramatic works. They are not set texts but may interest participants writing on the plays.

## 7. Glossary of key terms

| Term | Working meaning in the course |
|---|---|
| Arya | The monthly review (1914 to 1921) in which the essays first appeared |
| Artha | Wealth, interest and statecraft; one of the four aims of life |
| Assimilation | Taking in an outside influence and transforming it in one's own spirit |
| Dharma | Right law, duty and order; the frame for the other aims of life |
| Imitation | Taking in outside forms without one's own spirit |
| Kama | Desire, pleasure and beauty; one of the four aims of life |
| Moksha | Liberation; the spiritual aim of life |
| Purushartha | The aims of human life: dharma, artha, kama, moksha |
| Rasa | In Indian aesthetics, the flavour or emotion an audience tastes in a work |
| Religiosity | Religious forms kept without the spirit that gave them life |
| Renaissance | In his sense, the rebirth of a living spirit in new forms |
| Revival | The restoration of old forms |
| Spirit and form | A culture's inner life and the outward forms it creates |
| Spirituality | The direct seeking of the inner self and the infinite, as distinct from creed or rite |
| Svabhava | One's own nature, of a person or a people |
| Svadharma | The law of action and growth that follows from one's own nature |
| Time-Spirit | The pressure of an age that a living culture must answer |
| Varna | In his reading, an early ideal of social types and functions, distinct from hereditary caste |
