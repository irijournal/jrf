# Spirit and Form
## Capstone Guide

The capstone is each participant's answer to the question they drafted on Day 1, revised over six weeks. It has four parts: an essay, a presentation, a defence before a panel, and a part in a company's staged reading.

## 1. The four parts

| Part | Form | Weight |
|---|---|---|
| Essay | 3,000 to 4,000 words, due 72 hours after Day 30, revised in light of the defence | 20% |
| Presentation | Six minutes, Day 30, following the argument pyramid | 5% |
| Defence | Six minutes of questions from the panel and peers | 5% |
| Staged reading | A three-minute scene with a one-minute introduction, performed by a company of five | 10% |

## 2. Choosing the question

A good capstone question is answerable from the texts, arguable, and significant. Examples:

- Is Sri Aurobindo's distinction between renaissance and revival sound, and does it survive application to twentieth-century Indian culture?
- Does the Defence answer Archer's standard or only his facts?
- Can the ideal of varna in the Defence be separated from caste, as Ambedkar denied?
- How far does the essays' theory of art illuminate Indian sculpture at Sanchi or Khajuraho?
- Does Vasavadutta enact the Renaissance essays' idea of new forms from an old spirit?
- Is Perseus the Deliverer best read as political allegory?
- What do the five plays, taken together, say about love as a transforming power, and how does that bear on the essays?

## 3. The essay

| Section | Length | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| Introduction | 300 to 400 words | States the question, why it matters, and the thesis |
| Context | 300 to 500 words | Places the texts in their occasion, as far as the question needs |
| Argument | 1,800 to 2,400 words | Three claims, each with evidence from the texts and a counter-argument answered |
| Assessment | 300 to 500 words | Weighs strengths and limits; states what the thesis does not claim |
| Conclusion | 200 to 300 words | Returns to the question; names what remains open |
| Reflection (separate) | 500 words | The Day 1 and Day 30 sentences, and what changed them |

References: essay or act and scene, and page, in CWSA. Secondary sources in Chicago style. Length is not rewarded; precision is.

## 4. The presentation

Six minutes, following the argument pyramid: the thesis (one minute), three claims with their best evidence (four minutes), and what the thesis does not claim (one minute). At most three slides; one quotation per slide; every quotation referenced.

## 5. The defence

Six minutes of questions. The chair ensures at least one hard question for every participant. The panel assesses understanding, honesty and reasoning, not fluency alone. A clear concession is rewarded.

## 6. The staged reading

Each company of five chooses a scene on Day 27, prepares it on Day 28 and rehearses it on Day 29. On Day 30 one member introduces it in one minute: the interpretation in one sentence, and a link to the essays. The scene runs for three minutes. Assessment: the clarity of the interpretation and how the staging serves it (company mark), and the speaking of the verse (individual mark).

## 7. Running Day 30

At twelve minutes per presentation and defence plus a minute of changeover, and with 20 minutes of staged readings and 20 minutes for the opening and closing, a two-hour session holds about six presentations.

| Cohort | Time needed with one panel | With two parallel panels |
|---|---|---|
| Up to 6 | Two hours | Not needed |
| 12 | About 3.25 hours | About 2 hours |
| 16 | About 4 hours | About 2.5 hours |
| 20 | About 5 hours | About 3 hours |

Staged readings are performed once, to the whole cohort.

## 8. Panel briefing

The panel is the instructor and two guests: ideally a scholar of Sri Aurobindo and a critic or scholar from outside his tradition. Guests receive the rubric, the participant's thesis and essay draft 48 hours before Day 30. Panellists are asked to test the argument, not the participant's beliefs; to ask at least one question about evidence and one about a counter-argument; and to record a level on each rubric dimension before discussing.

## 9. Defence question bank

**On the question and thesis**
1. Why does this question matter?
2. What would prove your thesis wrong?
3. What does your thesis not claim?
4. How has your question changed since Day 1?

**On the texts**
5. Where exactly does he say that? Can you give the essay and the passage?
6. Is that his claim, or your inference from it?
7. Which essay complicates your reading?
8. How does the fact that the Defence is unfinished affect your argument?
9. Does the date of writing matter to your reading?

**On evidence**
10. Is that evidence or illustration?
11. What historical evidence supports that claim?
12. What would a historian add or dispute?
13. Which of his examples is weakest for your purpose?

**On critics**
14. What is the strongest objection to your thesis?
15. How would Archer reply?
16. How would Ambedkar reply?
17. Does Said's critique of Orientalism apply here?
18. Is your reading reverent or fair? How do you know?

**On the plays**
19. Is that in the play, or in his later philosophy read back into it?
20. What does the source tell you that the play changes?
21. Why that scene?
22. How would you stage the moment you quote?
23. What does the verse do in that passage?

**On significance**
24. What does your argument say about culture today?
25. Who would disagree, and why?
26. What would Sri Aurobindo say to your thesis?
27. What should a new reader of these texts read first, and why?
28. What question will you keep asking?

## 10. Submission checklist

- ☐ Essay, 3,000 to 4,000 words, with references to essay or act and scene, and page.
- ☐ Thesis stated in the introduction.
- ☐ At least one critic stated fairly and answered.
- ☐ Reflection (500 words) with the Day 1 and Day 30 sentences.
- ☐ Revision note responding to the defence.
- ☐ AI-use disclosure.
- ☐ Every quotation checked against the printed text.
