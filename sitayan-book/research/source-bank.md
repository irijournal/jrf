## Nabaneeta Dev Sen, "When Women Retell the Ramayan," Manushi no. 108 (1998): 18–27 (PDF from manushi.in)
- p. 18: "Epic poets the world over are men singing the glory of other men—armed men, to be precise."
- p. 18: four ways: (1) borrowing "the traditional eyes of the male epic poet, as Molla does"; (2) "with your own women's eyes, as Chandrabati does"; (3) "borrowing an ideological viewpoint as Ranganayakamma does"; (4) "tell your own story through the story of Sita, as the village women of India have been doing for hundreds of years."
- p. 18: "Molla, a woman and Shudra, threw a challenge to the Brahmin court poets by writing a perfect classical Ramayan." "The Brahmins did not allow Molla's work to be read in the royal court. And critics have rejected Chandrabati's as a weak and incomplete text."
- p. 18: "The village women care neither for the court nor for the critic—and they are not out to change the world."
- p. 19: "No one blesses a bride by saying, 'Be like Draupadi'."
- p. 19: "If patriarchy has used the Sita myth to silence women, the village women have picked up the Sita myth to give themselves a voice. They have found a suitable mask in the myth of Sita..."
- p. 19: "In the women's retelling, Sita is no rebel; she is still the yielding, suffering wife, but she speaks of her sufferings..."
- p. 19: "They call it the Ramayan but it is of Sita that they sing." favourite episodes from Balakanda and Uttarkanda, "the two so-called spurious books".
- p. 20: "The professional bard sings of Ram. The village woman sings of Sita." Ten sub-themes listed (foundling; marriage worry; child marriage; giving away; in-laws/abuse; golden deer/blaming the victim; need to bear a son; pregnancy cravings; childbirth; abandonment).
- p. 20: Bengali songs call Ram 'stone-hearted' and a 'sinner'; "Chandrabati calls Ram a deranged wimp".
- p. 20: Marathi song: "Dear Plum tree, dear Babul tree, Sita is telling you the story of her life... I was found at the tip of a plough / How can I have parents?"
- p. 21: epithet "Janam-dukhini" (born to suffer). Maithili song: Sita: "Listen father ... How much longer do you plan to keep me unmarried? This is not right behaviour!"
- p. 22: Telugu song: "The tiny girl is only as tall as seven jasmine flowers."; mother's advice list; Marathi: "Sita has been in exile right inside her bedroom"; "Sita's exile was right in her bedroom."
- p. 23: pregnancy cravings: "what does Sita, the individual, desire for herself?"; Marathi Ram laments losing a housekeeper; Sita to women: "'Ram has no compassion I am five months pregnant.'"
- p. 24: Telugu: crossing seven circles for seven sons; "she deserved it" mentality; "The fact that Sita was in fact not raped is a mere technicality."
- p. 25: Marathi: "Ram is reading about Sita's exile / In a book..."; Maithili birth-in-exile song "Alas! if only Ram would understand!"; "This is how women sing themselves into their Ramayan."
- p. 26: Marathi: "Because Sita is a woman ... Ram, just because some wild people talked / You have sent virtuous Sita into the forest!"; Bengali "pashanda", "papishthi"; "I have forgotten my face on Ram's bed."; "For Sita, as for Sarah, exile equals abandonment."; Marathi: "Sita's exile, / Let us share it among ourselves ... Sita distributed it amongst us all / Bit by bit."
- p. 27: grinding-stone song (women laugh at themselves); "She is a victim who suffers in grandeur, without being vengeful. She is a prey who never turns into a predator."; "So even in Chandrabati's poem, where Ram is severely criticised, Sita remains a docile wife while Chandrabati, the narrator, lashes her whip under her name."; final: "When women retell the Ramayan, Sita is the name they give themselves: the homeless female, the foundling, unloved, rejected and insecure."

## Madhu Kishwar, "Yes to Sita, No to Ram! The Continuing Popularity of Sita in India," Manushi no. 98 (Jan–Feb 1997): 20–31 (PDF from manushi.in)
- p. 20: Tulsi "marks the transition from Ram and Sita being presented as an ideal couple to projecting each of them as an ideal man and woman respectively."
- p. 20: "I grew up thinking of Sita as a much wronged woman — a slavish wife without a mind of her own."
- p. 20: "80 to 90 percent of the poems that came to us for the Hindi version of Manushi ... revolved around the mythological Sita"
- p. 21: her poem "Agnipariksha": "I too have given agnipariksha, / Not one — but many ... Who is Ram to reject me? / I have rejected that entire society / Which has converted / Homes into prisons."
- p. 22: contrast with "the popular western view" / "This linear view of human society makes the past something to be studied and kept in museums..."
- p. 22: "Indian women are not endorsing female slavery when they mention Sita as their ideal." "She is seen as a person whose sense of dharma is superior to and more awe inspiring than that of Ram"
- p. 22 (pull-quote, repeated p. 24): "Ram's rejection of Sita is almost universally condemned while her rejection of him is held up as an example of supreme dignity."
- p. 23: "People commonly perceive Sita's steadfastness as a sign of emotional strength and not slavery, because she refuses to forsake her dharma even though Ram forsook his dharma as a husband."
- p. 23: gods judged by present standards — "a far greater sense of freedom and volition to individuals within the Hindu faith than in religions where god's commandments are to be unconditionally obeyed"
- p. 23: agnipariksha seen "as an act of defiance"; refusal of second ordeal "interpreted not as an act of self annihilation but as a momentous but dignified rejection of Ram as a husband."
- p. 24: "Ram may not have rejected her as a wife but only as a queen in deference to social opinion, but Sita rejects him as a husband." Raghuvamsha 14.61 cited.
- p. 24: Avadh folk song (from Vidya Bindu Singh 1986): "The Ram who put me in the fire, who threw me out of the house / Guru, how shall I see his face?"; Luv-Kush: "We are the grandsons of Raja Janak and the beloved sons of Sita ... And we know not the name of our father."; "Sita descended into the earth, she spoke not a word."
- p. 25: Raveri (Yavatmal) Sita temple without Ram; Athavale apologia ("it was the king who abandoned the queen").
- pp. 26–27: Shetkari Sangathana, Lakshmi Mukti (1989) land-to-wives campaign; Sharad Joshi: "But then, how do we men treat our Lakshmis? Often no better than Ram treated Sita"; "a long overdue debt" to Sita mata.
- p. 28: Mithila: Sita a daughter of every household; people avoid marrying daughters in Margashirsha and to families west of Mithila.
- p. 29: "He is a Ram-like son, a Ram-like brother, or a Ram-like king." But they will never say ... "He is a Ram-like husband."; "Ram honge bade admi par Sita ne kya sukh paya?"; Sagar "was forced to deviate from his text and show the agnipariksha to be a mock one."
- p. 30: "The Hindu tradition might valourise wives who put up with tyrannical husbands gracefully but it does not valourise unreasonable husbands."
- p. 31: "By shaping themselves in the Sita mould, women often manage to acquire enormous clout and power over their husbands and family."
