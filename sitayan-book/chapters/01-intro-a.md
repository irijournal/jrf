# Introduction. Whose Sita? The Argument of This Book {.unnumbered}

## The scene at the sacrifice

The last time Vālmīki's Sita speaks, she is dressed in ochre. The detail is easy to miss, and most retellings miss it. Rama has performed the horse sacrifice at Naimiṣa; the twins Kuśa and Lava have sung the poem of his deeds before the assembled court; Vālmīki has brought Sita out of the forest and vouched for her, on the strength of his asceticism, that she is without sin. Rama has replied, in front of the whole world, that he knows the boys are his sons, that he knows Sita to be pure, that he abandoned her "out of fear of the people" (*lokabhayāt*), and that he would now be pleased if she would give proof of her purity "in the midst of the world".^[Vālmīki, *Rāmāyaṇa*, critical edition (hereafter CE) 7.88.3–4. All Sanskrit is cited from the Baroda critical edition as transcribed in GRETIL; translations are mine.] The gods arrive. A fragrant wind blows over the crowd. And then:

> *sarvān samāgatān dṛṣṭvā sītā kāṣāyavāsinī*\
> *abravīt prāñjalir vākyam adhodṛṣṭir avāṅmukhī*
>
> Seeing them all assembled, Sita, dressed in ochre, spoke with her palms joined, her eyes lowered, her face turned down.^[CE 7.88.9.]

What she says is an oath, not a plea: "As I have not thought of anyone but Rāghava, even in my mind, so may the goddess Mādhavī grant me an opening."^[CE 7.88.10.] The earth opens. A throne rises, borne on the heads of serpents. The Earth herself takes Sita in her arms, welcomes her, seats her, and the two of them sink out of sight while flowers fall from the sky and the gods cry "well done, well done".^[CE 7.88.11–15.] The poem records that "the whole world was bewildered for a moment".^[CE 7.88.20.]

I begin here because the scene contains, in compressed form, every problem this book is concerned with. The woman who speaks is dressed as an ascetic, not as a queen; she has, in the language of the tradition, already renounced. Her posture is the posture of deference: joined palms, lowered eyes, bowed face. Her words are an oath of fidelity so absolute that it extends to thought. And her act is a refusal. She does not undergo the second ordeal that Rama asks of her. She does not return to Ayodhya. She does not resume her place at his side. She leaves, and the manner of her leaving is so complete that it takes the earth with it.

How is this scene to be read? Is it the final submission of the perfect wife, who would rather die than live under suspicion, and whose last words are a declaration that her husband has been the whole of her inner life? Or is it the final refusal of a woman who has been tried twice, abandoned once while pregnant, and summoned back to be tried again, and who answers the summons by withdrawing herself from the jurisdiction of the court that summons her? The posture says one thing; the act says another. The words can be read both ways at once. The scene does not resolve the tension. It stages it.

Every modern writer who has reconstructed Sita has had to decide what to do with this scene, and with the scene before the fire in the sixth book, and with the scene on the bank of the Ganga where Lakshmana leaves her in the seventh. The decisions they have made, and the reasons they have given or concealed, are the subject of this book.

## The question

The question that drives this book is not mine alone; it was set out at the very beginning of the research plan from which the book grew. It may be stated as follows. Is Sita's literary afterlife fundamentally a history of women's changing possibilities of speech, or is it also a history of the changing political needs of the societies that keep rewriting her?^[The question is formulated in the project's research plan, "The Many Lives of Sita: Sita in the Modern Indian Literary Imagination", §8.]

The two halves of the question pull in different directions. The first half invites a story of progress. On this account the Sita of tradition was spoken for and spoken about; modern women writers, and some modern men, have given her the right to speak for herself; and the history of her retellings is therefore a chapter in the larger history of Indian women's struggle for voice, autonomy and recognition. The second half invites a story of use. On this account Sita has never belonged to herself. Every age has rewritten her to serve its own purposes, whether the purpose was the reformer's wish to produce a modern companionate wife, the nationalist's wish to produce a symbol of uncolonised Indian womanhood, the rationalist's wish to discredit a sacred text, the Marxist's wish to expose the class character of an ideal kingdom, the feminist's wish to recover a foremother, or the publisher's wish to sell a book. On this account, the history of Sita's retellings is not the history of a woman acquiring a voice. It is the history of a ventriloquist's dummy acquiring new ventriloquists.

I state the two positions starkly because each is held, in practice, by serious people, and because the most interesting work on Sita has always lived in the space between them. But I do not think the space between them is a neutral middle ground. It is my submission that the second account explains far more of the evidence than the first, that the first account is itself one of the political uses to which Sita has been put, and that the most powerful Sitas in the tradition, ancient and modern, are those that resist being used by either. They do so not by acquiring a voice, which Vālmīki's Sita already has in abundance, but by exercising a capacity the liberation narrative is poorly equipped to recognise: the capacity to refuse.

## Three errors of the liberation narrative

Let me be precise about what I am arguing against, since nothing is easier than to defeat an opponent of one's own invention. The liberation narrative, as I use the term, is not any single scholar's position. It is a set of assumptions that recur in the introductions to feminist retellings, in publishers' blurbs, in the reviews that greet each new Sita novel, in a large share of the journal articles that analyse those novels, and in the syllabi through which a generation of Indian students has been taught to read them. Its core claims can be summarised in three propositions. First, the traditional Sita is silent and submissive. Second, that silence and submission are the product of an ancient and continuous Brahmanical patriarchy. Third, modern retellings liberate Sita by giving her a voice, and the more voice they give her, the more feminist they are.

Each of these propositions is, in my view, mistaken, and the mistakes compound.

**The first error is textual.** Vālmīki's Sita is not silent. She argues with Rama about whether she will accompany him into exile, wins the argument, and wins it partly by taunting him; she asks what her father can have been thinking when he took as son-in-law "a woman in a man's form".^[CE 2.27.3: *kiṃ tvāmanyata vaidehaḥ pitā me mithilādhipaḥ / rāma jāmātaraṃ prāpya striyaṃ puruṣavigraham*.] She accuses Lakshmana of base motives in terms so harsh that she later regrets them.^[CE 3.43.20.] She defies Ravana, placing a blade of grass between them as she speaks, and tells him precisely how he will die.^[CE 3.54.1–9; repeated in captivity at CE 5.19.3.] She refuses Hanumān's offer of rescue on his back, because she will not voluntarily touch the body of any man other than Rama.^[CE 5.35.62.] When Rama, after the war, tells her before the assembled armies that he did not fight the war for her sake and that she may go where she pleases, she answers him point by point and accuses him of speaking "like a common man to a common woman".^[CE 6.104.5.] And when she is abandoned, pregnant, on the far bank of the Ganga, the message she sends back to the king is a lesson in kingship, not a lament.^[CE 7.47.10–12.] The scholarship has known all this for decades. Sally J. Sutherland Goldman's study of Sita's voice in the *Sundarakāṇḍa* is only the most careful of many demonstrations.^[Sally J. Sutherland Goldman, "The Voice of Sita in Vālmīki's *Sundarakāṇḍa*," in *Questioning Ramayanas: A South Asian Tradition*, ed. Paula Richman (Berkeley: University of California Press, 2001) (pinpoint to be verified) [W103].] What is remarkable is not that the evidence exists but that the liberation narrative has survived it.

**The second error is historical.** The silent, submissive Sita is real; I do not deny that she exists. But she is not timeless. She has a history, and that history is, to a far greater extent than is usually admitted, modern. She owes much to a devotional reorientation that began in the medieval period, in which the *Adhyātma Rāmāyaṇa* and Tulsīdās protected Sita's purity by substituting a shadow for her during the abduction, and in which, as Madhu Kishwar observes, the ideal couple of earlier tellings was replaced by an ideal man and an ideal woman considered separately.^[Madhu Kishwar, "Yes to Sita, No to Ram! The Continuing Popularity of Sita in India," *Manushi* 98 (January–February 1997): 20.] But she owes even more to the nineteenth century: to reformers who needed a model of wifely virtue that could be defended against missionary contempt, to nationalists who needed a symbol of an inner domain the coloniser could not touch, to the printing press and the oleograph that standardised her image, and to the cinema and television that fixed it. The Sita whom the liberation narrative sets out to liberate is, in substantial part, the Sita that colonial modernity produced. To treat her as the essence of "tradition" is to accept the coloniser's account of what Indian tradition was.

**The third error is conceptual.** The liberation narrative assumes that agency means voice, that voice means self-assertion, and that self-assertion means resistance to the institutions of marriage, family and religion. That chain of equivalences belongs to a particular history: the history of liberal individualism in the West. It is not false, but it is partial, and when it is applied without reflection to Sita it produces two distortions. It cannot see agency where agency takes forms other than self-assertion, such as the agency of a woman who keeps a vow, or who withdraws, or who refuses to plead. And it cannot see unfreedom where unfreedom takes the form of self-assertion, such as when a woman's newly granted voice is used to say precisely what the author, the publisher or the political movement needs her to say. The plan from which this book began puts the point exactly: "Sita's refusal need not look like modern liberal individualism to constitute agency."^[Research plan, §3.] Saba Mahmood made the same argument about a very different religious tradition, and her formulation will recur in these pages: agency is not "a synonym for resistance to relations of domination" but "a capacity for action that specific relations of subordination create and enable".^[Saba Mahmood, *Politics of Piety: The Islamic Revival and the Feminist Subject* (Princeton, NJ: Princeton University Press, 2005), 18† [W113].]

These three errors compound. If one begins with a silent Sita who never existed, attributes her to a tradition that did not produce her, and then measures her liberation by a yardstick borrowed from elsewhere, one ends by mistaking the newest political use of Sita for her emancipation. That, in my submission, is what has happened in a good deal of the writing about modern retellings, and it is what this book sets out to correct.

## Coloniality and the reading of Sita

A word is needed here about the vocabulary I shall use, because it is not yet common in Indian literary studies and because it may be mistaken for something it is not.

By *coloniality* I mean, following the Peruvian sociologist Aníbal Quijano and the Argentine semiotician Walter Mignolo, the pattern of power and knowledge that outlives formal colonial rule: the persistence, after the coloniser has left, of the coloniser's categories as the categories through which the formerly colonised understand themselves.^[Aníbal Quijano, "Coloniality of Power, Eurocentrism, and Latin America," *Nepantla: Views from South* 1, no. 3 (2000): 533–80; Walter D. Mignolo, "Epistemic Disobedience, Independent Thought and Decolonial Freedom," *Theory, Culture & Society* 26, nos. 7–8 (2009): 159–81.] Ashis Nandy, writing about India long before the decolonial school acquired its name, called the condition "the intimate enemy": a colonialism that "colonizes minds in addition to bodies".^[Ashis Nandy, *The Intimate Enemy: Loss and Recovery of Self under Colonialism* (Delhi: Oxford University Press, 1983), xi† .] J. Sai Deepak has argued at length that Indian constitutional and historical thought has yet to reckon with this condition, and that the recovery of what he calls an Indic consciousness requires a deliberate reading of Indian materials on their own terms before they are read through imported frames.^[J. Sai Deepak, *India That Is Bharat: Coloniality, Civilisation, Constitution* (New Delhi: Bloomsbury, 2021) (pinpoint to be verified).]

I find this framework useful for the study of Sita for a specific and limited reason. The figure who stands at the centre of the liberation narrative, the silent and submissive Sita, was assembled during the colonial encounter, partly by colonial administrators and missionaries who saw in her the degraded condition of the Hindu woman, and partly by Indian reformers and nationalists who answered them by making her the emblem of a superior spiritual womanhood. Both parties agreed on what she was; they disagreed only on whether to deplore her or to venerate her. When a modern feminist retelling sets out to liberate that Sita, it inherits the colonial agreement while reversing the nationalist valuation. It takes the coloniser's description and the reformer's anxiety and treats both as facts about the tradition. That is coloniality at work in the reading of a myth.

But I must be equally clear about what this framework does not license. It does not license the claim that all criticism of the Rāmāyaṇa's treatment of Sita is a colonial import. That claim is false, and the evidence against it is overwhelming. The women who sang, in Marathi, that "Sita's exile was right in her bedroom", the Bengali women who called Rama "stone-hearted" and "a sinner", the sixteenth-century poet Candrābatī who called him, in Nabaneeta Dev Sen's paraphrase, "a deranged wimp", and the Assamese tradition in which Sita calls him death incarnate, owed nothing to Mill or to Macaulay.^[Nabaneeta Dev Sen, "When Women Retell the Ramayan," *Manushi* 108 (1998): 20, 22; Tilottoma Misra, trans. and ed., *Sita's Voice in the Assamese Ramayana* (New Delhi: Zubaan, 2024) [W237].] The critique of Rama is indigenous. It is older than the colonial encounter and more radical than most of what the colonial encounter produced. Nor does the framework license the claim that the tradition is innocent of patriarchy, or of caste. The *Uttarakāṇḍa* that banishes Sita on the strength of public gossip also has Rama behead the Śūdra ascetic Śambūka for the crime of performing austerities.^[CE 7.67.2–4.] No reading of Sita that leaves Śambūka out of the frame deserves to be called decolonial, or indeed honest. Uma Chakravarti's demonstration that the control of women's sexuality was bound up with the maintenance of caste purity in early India is not a colonial slander; it is a historical argument from Indian sources, and it must be met on its own terms.^[Uma Chakravarti, "Conceptualising Brahmanical Patriarchy in Early India: Gender, Caste, Class and State," *Economic and Political Weekly* 28, no. 14 (3 April 1993): 579–85 [W079].]

The decolonial reading I shall attempt, then, is a double movement. It refuses the colonial description of the tradition as uniformly oppressive, and it refuses the nationalist defence of the tradition as uniformly benign. It insists that the resources for criticising Rama's treatment of Sita lie within the tradition itself, in Vālmīki's text, in the women's songs, in the regional poets, in the Jain and Buddhist tellings, and in the long practice of what Kishwar calls passing "moral judgements" on gods "in very human ways".^[Kishwar, "Yes to Sita," 23.] And it insists that those internal resources include the resources for criticising caste, since the Śambūka episode has been argued over within the tradition for as long as the banishment of Sita has. A reading that is faithful to the tradition is, for that very reason, a reading that can be severe with it.

## The argument in eight propositions

It may help the reader to have the argument of the whole book stated in advance, in the form of propositions that the chapters will defend. I shall return to them in the Conclusion and ask which of them have survived.

1. **Voice is not new.** The Sita of the Vālmīki *Rāmāyaṇa* speaks often, at length and with force; she argues, taunts, accuses, defies and judges. Any account of modern retellings that presents them as giving voice to a voiceless Sita begins from a false premise.

2. **Silence has a history.** The silent and submissive Sita is the product of identifiable historical processes: devotional reorientation in the medieval period, and, decisively, the colonial and nationalist construction of the ideal Hindu wife in the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, consolidated by print, oleograph, cinema and television.

3. **The critique of Rama is indigenous.** Criticism of Rama's treatment of Sita is as old as the tellings themselves. It appears in Vālmīki's own text, in Kālidāsa and Bhavabhūti, in Candrābatī, in the Assamese tradition, in Jain renunciation, and above all in the women's songs of every region. Modern feminist critique is a late entrant into an old argument, not its originator.

4. **Refusal, not voice, is Sita's signature.** The constant across the most powerful tellings is a series of refusals: the refusal to stay behind, the refusal of Ravana, the refusal of rescue on another man's terms, the refusal to plead, and finally the refusal of the second ordeal and of the return. Agency, in Sita's case, is most often exercised as withdrawal.

5. **Every modern Sita answers a political need.** The reformer's Sita, the nationalist's Sita, the rationalist's Sita, the Marxist's Sita, the liberal feminist's Sita, the Dalit feminist's Sita, the market's Sita and the Hindu nationalist's Sita each serve an identifiable programme. The more a telling gives Sita a voice, the more urgently one must ask whose sentences she is speaking.

6. **The feminist canon has its own exclusions.** The canon of "feminist retellings" is Sita-heavy, largely upper-caste and urban, and disproportionately written in English. It has been slow to hear Śabarī, Śambūka's kin, the Dalit woman labourer, and the Adivasi forest woman, and its idea of liberation is often the liberation of a queen.

7. **Vernacular women's narratives are intellectual history.** The songs sung by women at the grindstone, at weddings and in the fields are not "folklore" to be quarried for colour. They are a body of reflection on marriage, motherhood, labour, abandonment and justice, and they constitute an Indian women's intellectual tradition in their own right.

8. **The question is not whether Sita is liberated but from what, and into what.** A Sita liberated from the patriarchal household into the market, the nation or the party has changed masters, not acquired sovereignty. The most radical tellings are those that let Sita refuse every jurisdiction that claims her, including the jurisdiction of her liberators.

Propositions 3, 6 and 7 correspond closely to three of the four hypotheses with which the research began: that the domestic is a counter-epic (Hypothesis A), that Sita is not liberated simply by acquiring a voice (Hypothesis B), that the feminist canon has its own exclusions (Hypothesis C), and that vernacular women's narratives constitute intellectual history (Hypothesis D).^[The hypotheses are recorded in the corpus workbook, "Read Me" tab, and used as suggested tags throughout the Master Corpus.] Proposition 4 is, in effect, my answer to Hypothesis B: if voice does not liberate Sita, what does? Propositions 2 and 5 are the decolonial component of the argument, and they are the ones most likely to be contested.
