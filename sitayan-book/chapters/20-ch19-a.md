# Chapter 19. Laṅkā's Women: Mandodarī, Trijaṭā, Saramā and the Other Side of the War

::: {custom-style="Epigraph"}
*pāpānāṃ vā śubhānāṃ vā vadhārhāṇāṃ plavaṃgama*\
*kāryaṃ kāruṇyam āryeṇa na kaś cin nāparādhyati*

"Whether they are wicked or good, monkey, or even deserving death, a noble person should show compassion: there is no one who does not err."

— Sītā to Hanumān, refusing to let him punish her guards, Vālmīki *Rāmāyaṇa* 6.101.36 (CE)

## The argument of this chapter

Rama told Sita, before the fire, that the war had not been fought for her sake.^[CE 6.103.15.] It was fought, he said, for his manhood and his lineage's name. It was fought, too, against a city full of women: Ravana's queens and concubines, the wives and mothers of his soldiers, the *rākṣasīs* who guarded Sita in the Aśoka grove, the women who befriended her there. When Laṅkā burned and its king fell, they were the ones who were left. This chapter crosses the sea to them.

It makes five claims.

1. Vālmīki gives Laṅkā's women voices of remarkable range: Mandodarī's lament, which is at once grief, judgement and wounded pride; Trijaṭā's dream, which protects Sita from the other guards; Saramā's friendship, which risks her life for the captive. The epic's sympathy does not stop at the shore.

2. Sita's own treatment of her guards after the war, her refusal to let Hanumān punish them, is one of the most important and least discussed speeches in the poem. It is a jurisprudence of mercy, built on the recognition that the guards acted under command, and it is the precise opposite of the jurisprudence by which Sita herself is judged.

3. Mandodarī's lament contains the epic's most direct female judgement on male desire as the cause of war, delivered not by Sita but by her captor's wife.

4. Modern retellings have turned to Laṅkā's women, from Michael Madhusudan Dutt's Pramīlā and Saramā in 1861 to the Mandodarī novels of the 2010s and 2020s. Their turn is part of a larger reversal of sympathy that Chapter 5 traced, and it raises the same question: whose need does the rehabilitation of Laṅkā serve?

5. The women of Laṅkā are the clearest test in the epic of whether a feminist reading can extend across the line of a war. The epic itself, in Sita's mercy and Saramā's friendship, suggests that it can.

## Mandodarī: the lament of the enemy's queen

### The scene

When Ravana falls, the women of his household come out of the inner apartments onto the battlefield and lament over him. Among them, his chief queen, Mandodarī, "the eldest wife, beloved, wretched", looks at her husband slain by Rama and grieves.^[CE 6.99.1–2.] Her lament, in the critical edition, runs to some thirty verses, and it is one of the great laments of the epic.

It begins in disbelief. How can a man, a mere human wandering in the forest, have killed the lord of the three worlds, before whom even Indra trembled?^[CE 6.99.3–6.] Perhaps, she thinks, it was Indra himself in Rama's form; perhaps the destruction was already foretold when Khara fell at Janasthāna, when Hanumān entered the impregnable city.^[CE 6.99.10–12.] Then it turns to judgement:

> *kriyatām avirodhaś ca rāghaveṇeti yan mayā*\
> *ucyamāno na gṛhṇāsi tasyeyaṃ vyuṣṭir āgatā*
>
> When I told you, "Make peace with Rāghava," you would not accept it. This is the outcome.^[CE 6.99.13.]

She had counselled peace. He did not listen. He desired Sita "without reason, to the ruin of your power, your body and your kin".^[CE 6.99.14.] He did what was unworthy in assaulting a woman more honoured than Arundhatī and Rohiṇī.^[CE 6.99.15.]

### The pride in the lament

And then comes a verse that every modern Mandodarī novel has, in one way or another, had to reckon with:

> *na kulena na rūpeṇa na dākṣiṇyena maithilī*\
> *mayādhikā vā tulyā vā tvaṃ tu mohān na budhyase*
>
> Neither in family, nor in beauty, nor in courtesy is Maithilī superior to me, or even equal. But you, in your delusion, did not see it.^[CE 6.99.16.]

The verse is extraordinary. In the middle of her grief, over her husband's body, Mandodarī asserts her own worth against the woman for whom he died. She is not jealous in any simple sense; she has just called Sita more honoured than the most celebrated chaste wives of the heavens. She is wounded. Her husband preferred another woman, and the preference destroyed him, and she wishes it recorded that the preference was a delusion.

This is a woman's voice of a kind the epic rarely allows: a voice of self-regard. Sita's speeches, as Chapter 1 showed, are full of argument and defiance, but they are always about her fidelity, her conduct, her husband's injustice. Mandodarī, in this verse, speaks about herself: her family, her beauty, her accomplishments, her equality with or superiority to the woman her husband desired. Her grief is also the grief of a wife who was not chosen.

### Death and its sign

The lament's next verse names the cause of Ravana's death with a precision that is almost clinical:

> *sarvathā sarvabhūtānāṃ nāsti mṛtyur alakṣaṇaḥ*\
> *tava tāvad ayaṃ mṛtyur maithilīkṛtalakṣaṇaḥ*
>
> For every being, death does not come without a sign. Your death had Maithilī for its sign.^[CE 6.99.17.]

And then the contrast that gives the lament its bitterness: "Maithilī will roam with Rama, free of sorrow; but I, of little merit, have fallen into a terrible ocean of grief."^[CE 6.99.18.] Mandodarī knows, as she speaks, that the war has given Sita back to her husband and taken hers away. She does not know what the reader knows: that within a few cantos Sita will stand before the armies and be told the war was not for her, will walk into a fire, and will be banished years later on the strength of rumour. The enemy's queen envies a happiness that will not last.

### A woman's nature

Near the end of the lament, Mandodarī says something about herself that is easy to read as conventional self-deprecation and that is, I think, much more:

> *na hi tvaṃ śocitavyo me prakhyātabalapauruṣaḥ*\
> *strīsvabhāvāt tu me buddhiḥ kāruṇye parivartate*
>
> You, renowned for strength and manhood, are not one I should grieve for. But because of a woman's nature, my mind turns to compassion.^[CE 6.99.23.]

The logic of the warrior's ethic is that a hero who dies in battle is not to be mourned; he has achieved his end. Mandodarī knows the ethic and states it. And then she sets against it her own nature, a woman's nature, which "turns to compassion" despite it. The verse sets two ethics side by side: the warrior's, which forbids grief for a hero, and the woman's, which grieves anyway. And it attributes compassion to women as a nature, not a weakness.

Within three verses, Rama interrupts. He tells Vibhīṣaṇa to perform his brother's funeral rites, "and send these women back" (*striyaś caitā nivartaya*).^[CE 6.99.30.] The lament ends by royal command. The women of Laṅkā are returned to the inner apartments, and the poem moves on to the funeral, the consecration of Vibhīṣaṇa and the summoning of Sita.

### What Mandodarī knows

Read together, Mandodarī's lament contains three things that bear directly on the argument of this book.

The first is a female judgement on male desire as the cause of war. Mandodarī's diagnosis is precise: Ravana's death was caused by his desire for Sita, against counsel, "to the ruin of power, body and kin". In the epic's own economy, this is the counterpart of Rama's declaration before the fire that the war was fought for his manhood and his name. Both men fought for themselves. One fought for his desire, the other for his reputation. Mandodarī names the first; the fire god, in a sense, names the second.

The second is a woman's claim to her own worth. The verse in which Mandodarī says Sita was not her superior is the only moment in the epic in which a woman measures herself against Sita and declines to defer. Modern Mandodarī novels, as we shall see, have made this the centre of their heroine's character.

The third is the opposition between the warrior's ethic and compassion, which Mandodarī genders. This opposition runs through the whole of Laṅkā's women's story, and it reaches its fullest expression not in Mandodarī but in Sita, when the war is over and she is asked to punish her guards.

## Trijaṭā: the dream that protects

### The old guard

In the *Sundarakāṇḍa*, as the *rākṣasī* guards of the Aśoka grove threaten Sita with death and worse, one of them speaks against the rest. She is Trijaṭā, and the poem calls her old (*vṛddhā*); she has been lying down, and she rises to speak:

> *ātmānaṃ khādatānāryā na sītāṃ bhakṣayiṣyatha*
>
> Eat yourselves, ignoble ones; you will not eat Sita.^[CE 5.25.5.]

She has had a dream, terrible and hair-raising, which portends "the destruction of the *rākṣasas* and the prosperity of this woman's husband".^[CE 5.25.6.] The guards, frightened, ask her to tell it. She does: she saw Rama in a divine palanquin of ivory drawn by a thousand horses, and Sita dressed in white, seated on a white mountain surrounded by the sea, united with Rama "as light with the sun"; she saw Rama and Lakshmana on a great four-tusked elephant, and Sita climbing onto its shoulder.^[CE 5.25.10–14.] The dream continues with images of Ravana's fall.

### Prophecy as protection

Trijaṭā's dream is a prophecy, and it is also, unmistakably, a strategy. She speaks it at the moment when the other guards are threatening Sita, and its effect is to frighten them out of harming her. An old woman, among younger and more aggressive guards, uses the authority of a dream to protect the captive in her charge. She cannot command them; she can warn them. And the warning works.

This is a form of women's agency that the epic records without comment and that modern criticism has rarely noticed: the agency of the subordinate who protects the vulnerable by speaking in the register of prophecy, the only register in which her words will be heeded. Trijaṭā does not defy Ravana. She does not help Sita escape. She stands between Sita and the other guards with a dream, and the dream holds. In the vocabulary this book has used, it is agency within constraint of the most precise kind: the use of the one authority a powerless old woman possesses (the authority of the seer) to achieve what she could not achieve by command.

Chitra Banerjee Divakaruni's *The Forest of Enchantments* gives Trijaṭā and Saramā the role of Sita's companions in Laṅkā, and the corpus records the choice as one of the novel's significant departures.^[Chitra Banerjee Divakaruni, *The Forest of Enchantments* (Noida: HarperCollins India, 2019) [W169]; corpus workbook, W169.] The departure is, on the evidence of Vālmīki, less a departure than a recovery. The critical edition already gives Sita a protector among her guards.
