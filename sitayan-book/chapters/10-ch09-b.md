## "Do you accept my truth, my lord?": the women's theory of proof

### Two songs

Paula Richman's *Ramayana Stories in Modern South India* (2008), the anthology that made a whole regional literature of retelling available in English for the first time, includes two women's songs that deserve far more attention than they have received: a Telugu song, "Sita Locked Out", translated by Velcheru Narayana Rao, and a Kannada song, "Do You Accept My Truth, My Lord?", translated by Leela Prasad.^[Women's songs in Paula Richman, ed., *Ramayana Stories in Modern South India: An Anthology* (Bloomington: Indiana University Press, 2008): "Sita Locked Out" (Telugu), trans. Velcheru Narayana Rao; "Do You Accept My Truth, My Lord?" (Kannada), trans. Leela Prasad [W133]. Quotation is avoided; the account follows the corpus summary and must be checked against the anthology.] The corpus workbook describes them as "two women's songs that turn the epic into household quarrels and a demand that Rama accept Sita's word".^[Corpus workbook, W133.]

The first is a domestic comedy of the kind the women's tradition loves: a quarrel between husband and wife, a door shut against her, the ordinary friction of a marriage told through the most extraordinary of couples. It belongs with the songs Dev Sen collected in which Sita's exile takes place "right inside her bedroom", and with the Telugu song in which Sita, rescued from Laṅkā, covets a grinding stone and is shamed by Jāmbavān: "a song in which women laugh at themselves".^[Nabaneeta Dev Sen, "When Women Retell the Ramayan," *Manushi* 108 (1998): 22, 27.]

The second is, in my judgement, one of the most important texts in the corpus, because it articulates, in a single question, a theory of proof.

### The word against the fire

"Do you accept my truth, my lord?" The question is addressed by Sita to Rama, and its premise is that Sita's word is sufficient evidence of her truth. She does not offer to undergo an ordeal. She does not call on the gods. She asks whether he will accept what she says.

To see why this matters, it is necessary to recall how the Sanskrit legal tradition understood proof. The *dharmaśāstra*, the body of normative texts on law and conduct, distinguished between human means of proof and divine ones. The *Yājñavalkya Smṛti*, in a verse that later commentators treated as foundational, lists the human means (documents, possession and witnesses) and then states that "in the absence of any one of these, one of the divine proofs is prescribed".^[*Yājñavalkya Smṛti* 2.22†: *pramāṇaṃ likhitaṃ bhuktiḥ sākṣiṇaś ceti kīrtitam / eṣām anyatamābhāve divyānyatamam ucyate*. The verse number should be checked against a standard edition.] The divine proofs, the *divya*, included the ordeals of the balance, of fire, of water and of poison. They were a last resort, to be used when human evidence was wanting.

Measured by that standard, Rama's demand before the fire in Vālmīki's sixth book was not merely cruel; it was, in the tradition's own jurisprudence, irregular. Human evidence was not wanting. There was Sita's own testimony, which she offered on oath: "I swear to you by my own conduct."^[CE 6.104.6.] There was the testimony of Hanumān, who had seen her in the Aśoka grove and reported her fidelity. And there was, as Rama himself later admitted, his own knowledge: he "knew" she was devoted to him alone.^[CE 6.106.13.] The ordeal was resorted to not in the absence of evidence but in disregard of it, for the sake of what Rama called the "conviction of the three worlds".^[CE 6.106.14.] It was a public spectacle of proof, not a judicial necessity.

And notice what Vālmīki's Sita did with the fire. She did not call it a *divya*. She called it a witness: *lokasya sākṣī*, "the witness of the world".^[CE 6.104.24.] *Sākṣin*, witness, is precisely the term the *dharmaśāstra* uses for the human means of proof. Sita converted the divine ordeal into an act of testimony. She did not submit to the fire as a test; she called it to testify.

The Kannada women's song goes one step further. It dispenses with the fire altogether and asks Rama to accept the testimony of the only witness who was present at every moment of Sita's captivity: Sita herself. "Do you accept my truth?" is a demand that the woman's word be admitted as evidence. It is the demand that the whole ordeal tradition, from the epic's fire to the caste tribunal's interrogation, refused.

This is, I submit, a jurisprudential claim of the first importance, and it was made by women singing at home, in Kannada, without access to the *dharmaśāstra*'s technical vocabulary and without needing it. Hypothesis D holds that vernacular women's narratives constitute intellectual history. Here is a vernacular women's narrative that constitutes legal history: an argument about the evidentiary status of a woman's word, conducted against the background of an institution, the ordeal, that the women singing it knew from the epic and, in some communities, from life.

**Position A:** The ordeal vindicates Sita: by passing through the fire, she proves her purity beyond any doubt, and the proof protects her forever.\

**Where the evidence of this chapter leaves the argument.** The *dharmaśāstra* itself supports Position B more than its defenders admit, since it treats divine proof as a last resort when human evidence is lacking, and human evidence was not lacking. Vālmīki's Sita occupies a third position: she accepts the fire but renames it a witness (*sākṣī*, 6.104.24), converting ordeal into testimony. The Kannada song "Do You Accept My Truth, My Lord?" occupies a fourth: it rejects the fire and demands that her word be enough. That fourth position is, in modern terms, the principle that a woman's testimony about her own body is evidence. It was sung before it was legislated.

## Humour as critique: Kumudini's letters

The solemnity of the feminist retelling, from Asan's meditation through Volga's education to the first-person novels of the 2010s, can obscure a mode of critique that the women's tradition used freely and that the modern literature has largely abandoned: laughter.

Dev Sen's songs are full of it. Sita, rescued from Laṅkā, covets a grinding stone. Sita, pregnant, craves tiger's milk and a honeycomb from a teakwood tree on a sandbank in the middle of the ocean, and wants to eat the honey with *dosas*, and her mother-in-law snorts that she bore Rama and Lakshmana on green mangoes and coconut.^[Dev Sen, "When Women Retell," 23.] Rama, banished Sita gone, laments that there is no one left to sprinkle the floor or hand him his *dhotis*.^[Dev Sen, "When Women Retell," 23.] These songs laugh at Sita, at Rama, and at the singers themselves. The laughter is not a release from critique; it is a form of it. To laugh at Rama mourning his housekeeper is to name, with a precision no essay could match, what Sita's labour in the household was worth and how little it was valued until it was gone.

The corpus records a written version of this mode in Tamil. Kumudini, a Tamil woman writer of the mid-twentieth century, wrote satirical "Letters from Lady Sita", in Sita's voice, which Richman's anthology translates.^[Kumudini, "Letters from Lady Sita," in Richman, *Ramayana Stories in Modern South India* [W126]; year and author details to be checked.] The corpus workbook notes them as evidence of "humour as a women's mode of critique, a different route from lament".^[Corpus workbook, W126.] A Sita who writes letters with a satirist's eye is a Sita who stands outside her own story and comments on it: not from the solemn retrospect of Asan's riverbank, but from the vantage of a woman who finds the whole arrangement absurd.

Humour has a political advantage that lament lacks. Lament invites sympathy; sympathy is compatible with leaving things as they are. Laughter invites recognition: the listener who laughs at Rama mourning his housekeeper has recognised the housekeeper's labour. And laughter is hard to censor, because it can always deny that it meant anything. The Tamil satirist and the Marathi singer could say, in jest, what a novelist saying it in earnest might be threatened for. That the modern feminist retelling, with few exceptions, has given up this mode in favour of earnest first-person testimony is a loss, and it is, I think, connected to the market in which that retelling now circulates: earnestness sells to a readership that wants to be moved; satire sells less well.

## The southern poets: asking Sita, writing to Sita

Richman's anthology also brings together a group of modern poems in Malayalam, Kannada and Tamil that address Sita or speak about her, and they show the range of the southern response in a small compass.

The Kannada feminist poet Vijaya Dabbe (1952–2020) wrote "Asking Sita", in which the poet addresses her own questions to Sita: the modern woman interrogating the ancient one about the choices she made.^[Vijaya Dabbe, "Asking Sita," in Richman, *Ramayana Stories in Modern South India* [W119].] The Malayalam poet Lalitha Lenin wrote "In the Shadow of Sita", a woman poet's reflection on living under the Sita ideal.^[Lalitha Lenin, "In the Shadow of Sita," in Richman, *Ramayana Stories in Modern South India* [W122].] And the Malayalam poet K. Satchidanandan wrote "Come Unto Me, Janaki", a poem in the form of Ravana's letter to Sita: desire addressed to her by the epic's villain.^[K. Satchidanandan, "Come Unto Me, Janaki," in Richman, *Ramayana Stories in Modern South India* [W120].]

The three poems map three relations to Sita. Dabbe's is interrogative: the modern woman questions the ideal, as a daughter might question a mother whose choices she cannot understand. Lenin's is existential: the ideal is a shadow under which the poet lives, a presence that falls across her own life. Satchidanandan's is erotic and transgressive: a male poet writing Ravana's desire for Sita, which the corpus workbook suggests comparing with Meena Kandasamy's poems in which Sita herself desires Ravana.^[Corpus workbook, W120; see Chapter 10.] The comparison is instructive. When a male poet writes Ravana's desire for Sita, Sita remains the object of desire, now addressed by a more ardent lover. When a woman poet, in Chapter 10, writes Sita's desire for Ravana, Sita becomes the subject. The difference between the two is the difference between a new admirer and a new agent.

## Ambai's forest

The Tamil writer Ambai (C. S. Lakshmi, born 1944), novelist, short-story writer and founder of the archive of women's voices SPARROW in Mumbai, wrote a story titled "Forest" (in Tamil, *Kāṭu*), of which Richman's anthology includes an excerpt.^[Ambai, "Forest," excerpt in Richman, *Ramayana Stories in Modern South India* [W121]; year and full source to be checked.] The corpus record describes it as a story that "brings Sita and the forest into a woman's search for selfhood".^[Corpus workbook, W121.] Geetanjali Mahajan's essay reads it alongside Volga as an instance of Sita's agency expressed as a quest for selfhood that stops short of revolution.^[Geetanjali Mahajan, "Rebel not Revolutionary: Sita's Agency and Quest for Selfhood," *The Criterion* 8, no. 6 (2017) [W158].]

Ambai's choice of the forest as the site of a modern woman's search for self connects her story to every theme of this chapter and the last. The forest is where Sita was exiled and where Volga's Sita met the women who liberated her; it is where Meher's Sita became an ascetic and where Aravindan's Sita became the world; it is where Śabarī's knowledge lived. For a modern Tamil woman, in Ambai's story, the forest is the place outside the household where a self might be found. Ambai's founding of an archive of women's voices is not incidental to this. Her life's work has been the preservation of counter-memory: the records that the official archive does not keep.

## The Mappila Rāmāyaṇa and the women performers

Two further southern traditions complete the picture, and both complicate any account of the Rāmāyaṇa as a Hindu text in a narrow sense.

The *Mappila Rāmāyaṇa*, a playful, localised song version of the story from the Muslim Māppiḷa community of Malabar, collected from the singer Hassankutty by M. N. Karassery and translated in Richman's anthology, retells the epic in the idiom of Malabar Muslim life.^[*Mappila Ramayana*, sung by Hassankutty, collected by M. N. Karassery, in Richman, *Ramayana Stories in Modern South India* [W127].] Its Sita and its Śūrpaṇakhā are figures of a world in which the epic was a shared inheritance across religious lines. And in Kerala's temple theatre, the women's tradition of *Naṅṅyārkūttu*, performed by the Naṅṅyār women of the Nambiar community, has been studied by Mundoli Narayanan as a form in which women performers shape the narrative and write their own selves into it.^[Mundoli Narayanan, "Writing Her 'Self': The Politics of Gender in Nangyarkuttu," in *Performing the Ramayana Tradition: Enactments, Interpretations, and Arguments*, ed. Paula Richman and Rustom Bharucha (New York: Oxford University Press, 2021) [W179].]

Both traditions are evidence for the corpus workbook's Field I, "before modern feminism: women's counter-narratives", and Field VII, "reception, religion and contemporary politics". The Mappila songs show that counter-memory need not be oppositional: they remember the epic in a community's own idiom, without hostility. The Naṅṅyār performers show that women's authorship of the Rāmāyaṇa was not confined to the songs of the courtyard; it existed, within strict limits, in the temple itself.

## Conclusion

The southern retellings of the 1990s and 2000s, and the women's songs that scholars translated in the same years, developed a method distinct from Volga's: the monologue as counter-memory. Sarah Joseph used it to give voice to Mantharā and Śūrpaṇakhā, the women the epic most consistently blamed, and in doing so extended feminist sympathy, haltingly, across class. The Kannada women's song "Do You Accept My Truth, My Lord?" used the form of a single question to articulate a theory of proof that rejects the ordeal and admits a woman's word as evidence. Kumudini's letters and the songs of the grindstone used laughter as critique. The poets asked Sita questions, lived in her shadow, and wrote her letters. Ambai sent a modern woman into the forest to look for herself.

What these works share is an insistence on the particular. They do not liberate Sita in general; they remember particular women, particular quarrels, particular injustices, particular evidence. Chapter 10 asks whose particulars they are, and turns to the writers who insist that the feminist canon, for all its achievements, has remembered some women and forgotten others.
