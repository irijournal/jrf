## What kind of liberation?

### Not voice: knowledge

The first thing to say about Volga's liberation is what it is not. It is not the acquisition of a voice. Volga's Sita does not, in the course of the book, learn to speak; she was never silent. She does not deliver a great speech of self-assertion against Rama; the book has no such scene. What she acquires is knowledge, and the knowledge comes from other women.

This is a structural decision of the first importance, and it aligns Volga with the oldest layer of the women's tradition rather than with the liberal novel. In Chapter 2, I proposed the term *choral agency* for the way the women's songs distribute the case for the wronged woman among a community of voices: the singer, the trees, the women of the household. Volga's book is choral agency rendered as narrative. Each woman Sita meets contributes a part of what she comes to understand; none of them alone could have liberated her; and the liberation, when it comes, belongs to all of them. The Marathi song that Dev Sen quoted, in which Sita, leaving for the forest, distributed her exile "amongst us all, bit by bit", is the precise inverse of Volga's design: there, Sita distributes her suffering among the women; here, the women distribute their understanding to Sita.^[Nabaneeta Dev Sen, "When Women Retell the Ramayan," *Manushi* 108 (1998): 26.]

Hypothesis B, that Sita is not liberated simply by acquiring a voice, receives in Volga its strongest positive confirmation. Volga's answer to the question "what liberates Sita?" is: other women's experience, understood. Geetanjali Mahajan's phrase for Volga's Sita, and for Ambai's, is apt: "rebel, not revolutionary".^[Geetanjali Mahajan, "Rebel not Revolutionary: Sita's Agency and Quest for Selfhood," *The Criterion* 8, no. 6 (2017) [W158].] She does not overthrow the order; she understands it, and stops consenting to it.

### Refusal

The second thing to say is that the knowledge culminates in refusal. Whatever the precise shape of the book's ending, its trajectory is towards Sita's withdrawal of consent from Rama's world: from the tribunal Ahalya taught her to see through, from the patrilineal claim Reṇukā taught her to fear, from the marriage Urmila taught her to leave. Volga's Sita is liberated not when she speaks against Rama but when she no longer needs his verdict.

This is the claim this book has made about Vālmīki's Sita in Chapter 1, about Pudumaippittan's Ahalya in Chapter 6 and about the Avadh women's song in Chapter 2. Volga's book is its most sustained modern statement. The difference is that Volga makes the refusal the outcome of a process of learning, rather than an act of will at a single decisive moment. In Vālmīki, Sita refuses in one sentence, at the assembly. In Volga, she refuses at the end of a long education. The education is the book.

### Solidarity, and its limits

The third thing to say is that the solidarity which educates Volga's Sita has a membership, and the membership is not unlimited.

The women Sita meets in *The Liberation of Sita* are Śūrpaṇakhā, a *rākṣasī* princess, Ravana's sister; Ahalya, the wife of a Brahmin sage; Reṇukā, the wife of another Brahmin sage and mother of a Brahmin warrior; and Urmila, a princess of Mithila and Ayodhya. Only Śūrpaṇakhā comes from outside the epic's upper registers, and she is royal in her own world. The women Sita does not meet include Śabarī, the forest woman whose tasted berries Rama accepted; the *rākṣasī* guards of the Aśoka grove; the servants of Ayodhya, Mantharā among them; and any woman of the agricultural or labouring castes.

I do not raise this to diminish the book, which is a work of the first rank. I raise it because the research plan for this study insisted that we ask "whose Sita is being universalised", and because the answer, for the founding text of modern feminist retelling, is: a Sita whose sisters are, with one exception, the wives and daughters of kings and sages.^[Research plan, §2, on Sharmila Rege.] The corpus workbook's idea log names the gap as a finding: "the P1 list is Sita-heavy and mostly upper-caste; Shabari, Shambuka and Dalit women's voices are few".^[Corpus workbook, My Ideas tab, idea 3.] Volga's own later work suggests that she saw it too.

### On the Banks of the Pampā: the forest woman's knowledge

In 2025, HarperCollins India published in English Volga's novel about Śabarī, *On the Banks of the Pampa*.^[Volga, *On the Banks of the Pampa* (Noida: HarperCollins India, c. 2025) [W234]; the year of the Telugu original is to be checked.] The corpus records it as "an ecofeminist, anti-caste Shabari", and a review in *Open* describes it as the last part of Volga's feminist epic trilogy.^[Corpus workbook, W234.] Its Śabarī is an Adivasi forest woman whose knowledge of the forest is set against the learning of the Brahmin scholars; the sage Mataṅga's hermitage and the Pampā lake are her world.^[Corpus workbook, W234.]

The choice is pointed, and its significance for the argument of this book is considerable. In Vālmīki and still more in the devotional tradition, Śabarī is a figure of devotion: an old forest woman of low birth who waits for years for Rama, offers him berries she has tasted to make sure they are sweet, and is rewarded with release. Philip Lutgendorf's study of the episode across tellings shows how the tasted berries have been read as an emblem of devotion's power to dissolve purity rules, and how that emblem has been put to very different uses, from Gandhian anti-untouchability to the Hindutva mobilisation of Adivasis at the Shabari Kumbh in the Dangs in 2006.^[Philip Lutgendorf, "Dining Out at Lake Pampa: The Shabari Episode in Multiple Ramayanas," in *Questioning Ramayanas*, ed. Paula Richman (Berkeley: University of California Press, 2001) [W098]; on the Shabari Kumbh and the Adivasi version in which Shabari enters the fire instead of Sita, corpus W159.] Volga's Śabarī is neither a devotee awaiting the god nor a symbol for a political campaign. She is a knower: a woman whose knowledge of plants, seasons, water and animals is a form of learning that the hermitage's Sanskrit scholarship cannot recognise.

The novel thus extends Volga's solidarity of the wronged to the person the first book omitted, and it changes the nature of the solidarity in doing so. In *The Liberation of Sita*, what the women share is the experience of having been wronged by the patriarchal order. In *On the Banks of the Pampa*, what the forest woman has is knowledge of a world outside that order altogether: a forest that is not the kingdom's margin but its own centre. The movement is from a feminism of grievance to a feminism of alternative knowledge, and it brings Volga into the debate between Vandana Shiva and Bina Agarwal that Chapter 6 traced. Whether her Śabarī's forest knowledge is an essentialised feminine principle or a materially grounded practice is a question the novel must answer in its detail, and I leave it to the reader with the edition.

## The renunciant's exit

I want now to make an argument about Volga that, so far as I know, has not been made, and that bears directly on the decolonial claims of this book.

The liberation that Volga's Sita achieves has the shape of renunciation. She learns, from women who have passed beyond the economy of marriage, that the attachments which bound her (to Rama's verdict, to her sons as Rama's heirs, to her place in the royal household) are the source of her captivity, and she frees herself by releasing them. Her freedom is a freedom from attachment. Volga's subsequent novel about Yaśodharā, the wife whom the Buddha left, suggests how deeply the theme of renunciation runs through her thought; Kumari Ruchi and Smita Jha have read the two books together as voices recovered from the margins.^[Kumari Ruchi and Smita Jha, "Reinventing Marginalized Voices: A Study of Volga's *The Liberation of Sita* and *Yashodhara*," *Journal of International Women's Studies* 25, no. 5 (2023), article 2 [W188].]

Now recall the ending of the Jain *Paumacariya* of Vimalasūri, discussed in Chapter 1. After the ordeal, Sita does not return to Rama, and she does not enter the earth. She takes *dīkṣā*, initiation as a Jain nun, and other women follow her into renunciation.^[Vimalasūri, *Paumacariya* [W004].] The corpus workbook calls this "renunciation as Sita's own exit: a non-Hindu telling where she chooses her ending".^[Corpus workbook, W004.] Recall, too, that Vālmīki's Sita comes to her final scene in ochre, *kāṣāyavāsinī*, the colour of the renunciant.^[CE 7.88.9.]

The most celebrated modern feminist liberation of Sita thus rejoins one of the oldest sectarian tellings of her story, and echoes a detail of Vālmīki's own last scene that most readers overlook. This is not a criticism of Volga. It is evidence for this book's third proposition: that the resources for Sita's liberation lie within the tradition, and that the moderns who find them are, knowingly or not, rediscovering exits the tradition had already imagined. Volga's Sita leaves Rama the way the Jain Sita left him: by renouncing the world in which he could judge her. What is modern in Volga is not the exit but the route to it, through the solidarity of wronged women; and even that route, as Chapter 2 showed, was mapped in the songs.

The decolonial point follows. The liberation narrative assumes that the resources for Sita's freedom come from modernity: from feminism, from liberal individualism, from the vocabulary of rights. Volga's book, which that narrative claims as its showpiece, finds its resources in the women's songs, in the classical figures of Ahalya and Reṇukā, and in the renunciant's exit. Its feminism is indigenous in its materials, even where its vocabulary is modern. That is not a weakness; it is the source of its power. A Sita liberated by Mill would have nowhere to go. Volga's Sita has the whole tradition's forest.

## Rama, again

I return to the question deferred at the end of the first half of this chapter: Volga's compassion for Rama.

The objection is serious. If Rama is himself a prisoner of his role, then the structure that imprisoned Sita has no author; it simply exists, and everyone is its victim. A feminism that ends by pitying the king risks dissolving the very responsibility that Chapter 1 located so precisely in the text: Rama's decision, without a hearing, on the basis of public precedent, to banish a pregnant wife he knew to be innocent. Vālmīki's Rama confesses that he knew; Volga's Rama, understood with compassion, risks being excused.

But the objection can be answered, and I think the answer is in the book's design. Volga's compassion for Rama comes at the end of Sita's education, not at the beginning. It is the compassion of the liberated, not of the captive. A captive who pities her captor is in danger; she may excuse what is done to her. A woman who has walked out of the prison and then turns to look at the jailer, still inside, has earned the right to see him clearly, and what she sees is a man who cannot leave. That is not an excuse. It is a judgement of a different kind: the judgement that the king, for all his power, is less free than the woman he banished. It is, in its way, the final reversal of the trial. The accused walks free; the judge remains in the dock of his own role.

Kishwar's evidence suggests that ordinary women had reached this judgement long before Volga. "Ram honge bade admi par Sita ne kya sukh paya?" ("Ram may have been a great man, but what good did it do Sita?"), her informants asked; and "Ram may not have rejected her as a wife but only as a queen in deference to social opinion, but Sita rejects him as a husband."^[Madhu Kishwar, "Yes to Sita, No to Ram! The Continuing Popularity of Sita in India," *Manushi* 98 (January–February 1997): 29, 24.] The judgement that Rama was a prisoner of his kingship, and that Sita was freer for leaving him, is a judgement that the women of North India had expressed in their own idiom. Volga gave it narrative form.

## The price of retelling

Volga's work has not been received with universal acclaim, and the response to it shows what is at stake in retelling Sita in the twenty-first century. In December 2016, she published in the Telugu newspaper *Andhra Jyothi* a story called "Aśōkaṃ", about a sympathetic Mandodarī; the corpus records that it "drew threats against the author".^[Volga, "Asokam," *Andhra Jyothi* (December 2016) [W154]; the text has not yet been located.] The story is minor in her work; the response was not. A woman writer, already honoured by the Sahitya Akademi for a book in which Sita is liberated through the solidarity of wronged women, was threatened for imagining Ravana's wife with sympathy.

I note the episode not to make a martyr of Volga, who would reject the role, but because it places her book in the history traced in Figure 5.2: the history of tellings that provoke public storms. That history shows a clear pattern. The tellings that diminish Rama, or that extend sympathy to those the epic casts as his enemies, are the ones that draw threats. Volga's Mandodarī story joined a line that runs from Menen's banned satire through the Periyar translation and Ambedkar's appendix to *Sita Sings the Blues* and Ramanujan's essay. The liberation of Sita is permitted. The rehabilitation of Lanka is not.

## Conclusion

*The Liberation of Sita* is the book in which the modern feminist retelling of Sita came of age, and it deserves its place at the centre of the field. But its achievement is not the one usually attributed to it. It does not give Sita a voice; she always had one. It gives her an education, conducted by women, culminating in refusal; and in doing so it rejoins, through the solidarity of the wronged and the exit of the renunciant, two of the oldest resources of the Indian tradition: the women's songs and the Jain Sita who took initiation. Its limit, which Volga herself addressed in her later novel about Śabarī, is the membership of its sisterhood.

Chapter 9 turns south, to the Malayalam and Tamil women whose retellings were contemporary with Volga's and who chose a different method: not the encounter but the monologue, not the education of Sita but the counter-memory of the women the epic forgot.
