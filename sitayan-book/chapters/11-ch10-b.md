## Meena Kandasamy: desire and anger

### The poet and her publisher

Meena Kandasamy (born 1984), poet, novelist and activist, published her second collection of poems, *Ms Militancy*, in 2010 with Navayana, the Delhi publishing house devoted to anti-caste literature.^[Meena Kandasamy, *Ms Militancy* (New Delhi: Navayana, 2010) [W136].] The publisher is part of the argument. Navayana's name comes from Ambedkar's Buddhism; its list is built around Ambedkar, Periyar and the literature of caste. Kandasamy's poems about Sita and Śūrpaṇakhā were not issued by a mainstream house looking for a feminist retelling to place beside its mythological bestsellers. They were issued by a press whose programme is the annihilation of caste, and they announce their allegiance.

The corpus records four elements of the collection that bear on this book. In the preface, Kandasamy writes that "My Sita climbs onto a stranger's lap."^[Kandasamy, *Ms Militancy*, preface, as recorded in the corpus workbook, W136 (page to be verified).] In the poem "Princess-in-Exile", Sita "perfected the vanishing act".^[Kandasamy, "Princess-in-Exile," in *Ms Militancy*, as recorded in the corpus workbook, W136 (page to be verified).] In the poem "Random Access Man", Sita chooses Ravana out of desire. And in "Traitress", the poet speaks for Śūrpaṇakhā.^[Corpus workbook, W136; Gunja Patni's reading of the Ramayana poems in *MZU Journal* 6, no. 2 (2020) [W172].]

### The stranger's lap

"My Sita climbs onto a stranger's lap." The sentence is an act of deliberate desecration, and its force depends on the reader's knowledge of the text it desecrates. In Vālmīki, the citizens of Ayodhya say of Sita that she was "carried off by force by Ravana on his lap" (*aṅkam āropya hi purā rāvaṇena balād dhṛtām*), and ask what pleasure Rama can take in her.^[CE 7.42.17.] Rama himself, before the fire, asks how he could take back a woman "fallen from Ravana's lap" (*rāvaṇāṅkaparibhraṣṭām*).^[CE 6.103.20.] Ravana's lap is the image of Sita's contamination: the place where her body was, against her will, and from which, in the eyes of the city, she can never be cleansed. Vālmīki's Sita answered that her body had been in another's power and her heart in her own.^[CE 6.104.8–9.]

Kandasamy's Sita *climbs* onto the lap. The verb restores to her the will that Vālmīki's Sita said she lacked in Laṅkā, and directs it to the very act that the city held against her. The stranger is unnamed; it need not be Ravana. What matters is that she chooses, and that what she chooses is the thing that the ordeal was designed to disprove. The sentence does not defend Sita against the charge. It pleads guilty and denies that the charge is a crime.

This is a move of a different order from the rehabilitation of Śūrpaṇakhā or the education of Volga's Sita. It is closest, among the texts this book has examined, to Chalam's 1924 play, in which Sita walks into Ravana's pyre. But the difference is decisive. Chalam's Sita chose Ravana because Ravana respected her; her choice was a verdict on the two men. Kandasamy's Sita climbs onto a stranger's lap because she wants to; her choice is a verdict on nothing but her own desire. And the voice that makes the choice is a woman's, writing in her own name, about a Sita she calls hers: "*My* Sita".

### The vanishing act

"Princess-in-Exile" gives the reverse image. Sita "perfected the vanishing act". The phrase reads the whole of Sita's story, from the abduction to the earth, as a series of disappearances, and reads her final disappearance as a skill: the act of a woman who has learned, from long practice, how to remove herself from a world that will not stop judging her. It is the refusal this book has traced from Vālmīki onward, described with a conjuror's irony. The earth that opens is not a miracle; it is a trick she has been practising.

The two images, the climbing and the vanishing, are the two poles of Kandasamy's Sita: desire and withdrawal, the body that chooses and the body that leaves. Neither is the tested body of the ordeal. Both refuse the ordeal's premise, that a woman's body is a site of proof.

### The traitress

And "Traitress" speaks for Śūrpaṇakhā. Kandasamy's anti-caste politics makes her Śūrpaṇakhā a figure not of family dysfunction but of the boundary that Chapter 10's first half identified: the forest woman mutilated by Aryan princes for approaching them across the line. In a collection published by an Ambedkarite press, Śūrpaṇakhā is restored to the political logic that the market's victim rehabilitation detached her from. She is not an unloved daughter; she is a woman of the people whose land the princes came to clear, and her desire is a transgression of a caste boundary, punished as such.

### A declared programme

Kandasamy's poems are, in my judgement, among the most important feminist interventions in the corpus, precisely because they refuse every element of the ideal Sita and the ideal Śūrpaṇakhā at once: chastity, patience, the tested body, the victim. But this book has asked of every retelling whether it answers a political need, and it must ask it of Kandasamy too.

It does. Kandasamy's Sita who desires Ravana belongs to the Periyarite lineage in which Ravana is the wronged Dravidian king and Rama the Aryan aggressor; her Sita's choice of the "stranger" over Rama is a choice of the anti-caste hero over the Brahmanical one. The corpus workbook calls the collection "an anti-caste feminist poetics of desire and anger that breaks the chaste-Sita script".^[Corpus workbook, W136.] Both halves of the description are accurate. The desire is Sita's, and it is also the movement's.

The difference between Kandasamy and the anti-epic writers of Chapter 5 lies not in the presence of a programme but in its declaration. Chalam presented his Sita's choice as her own verdict on two men, concealing the fact that the verdict was Chalam's. Periyar presented his reading as the "true" Rāmāyaṇa, concealing its colonial genealogy. Kandasamy writes "*my* Sita". The possessive declares that the Sita of the poems is the poet's, and that the poet's politics are in her. A declared programme is not an absence of programme. But it is an honesty about it, and in the long history of Sita's borrowing that this book has traced, honesty about the loan is rare.

## Hira Bansode: the question to Śabarī

The Marathi Dalit poet Hira Bansode (born 1939) addressed a poem to Śabarī, and it is the most direct challenge in the corpus to the devotional reading of the episode of the tasted berries.^[Hira Bansode, "Shabari" [W059]; year to be checked; see Saloni Walia, "Contextualizing Hira Bansode's 'Shabari'," *Setu* (October 2018) [W162].] In the corpus's summary, the poet asks Śabarī why she offered Rama berries instead of asking him about Ekalavya's thumb and Sita's exile.^[Corpus workbook, W059.]

The question is a masterpiece of compression. Śabarī, in the devotional reading, is the low-caste woman whose devotion is rewarded: Rama accepts her tasted berries, and the acceptance shows that devotion overcomes the rules of purity. Bansode refuses the reward. Why, she asks, did Śabarī offer Rama anything at all? Why did she not ask him, instead, about Ekalavya, the tribal archer of the *Mahābhārata* whose teacher Droṇa demanded his right thumb as a fee so that he could never rival the princes; and about Sita, the wife Rama would banish? The poem links three injustices (the low-caste woman's subservience, the tribal boy's mutilation, the upper-caste wife's banishment) and asks the oppressed to stop feeding the god who presides over all three.

What Bansode does here is precisely what this chapter has argued the feminist canon has failed to do: she reads the three regimes together. Sita's exile appears in her poem not as the central injustice of the epic but as one of several, set beside a caste injustice and a tribal one, and addressed to the one figure in the Rāmāyaṇa who stands outside the upper-caste household and inside the forest. The question is put to Śabarī, not to Sita, because it is Śabarī who has the standing to ask it.

The episode of the berries has, in the twenty-first century, acquired a further political charge. A feature in *Scroll*, recorded in the corpus, traces Śabarī's image from the paintings of Nandalal Bose and Atul Dodiya to the Shabari Kumbh held in the Dangs district of Gujarat in 2006, a Hindu nationalist mobilisation of Adivasis organised around her figure, and records an Adivasi version of the story in which it is Śabarī, not Sita, who enters the fire.^[Corpus workbook, W159 (author and year of the *Scroll* feature to be checked).] The Adivasi version is the most startling inversion in the corpus: the forest woman takes upon herself the queen's ordeal. It suggests that the communities who were told the story of Śabarī's devotion understood perfectly well who, in the real distribution of suffering, was likely to be put through the fire.

## *Sannathimmi's Ramayana*: the labourer's Sita

The corpus includes one text that the workbook regards as "a rare Dalit feminist retelling by a Dalit woman writer", and that fills its Field IV gap: *Sannathimmi's Ramayana*, by the Kannada writer and activist Du. Saraswathi.^[Du. Saraswathi, *Sannathimmi's Ramayana* [W219]; the year, and whether it is a printed book or a performance text, are to be checked. The author's English version circulates as a PDF.] Its narrator, Sannathimmi, is a Dalit woman labourer who tells the Rāmāyaṇa in her own words. Her Sita, in the corpus's summary, "is a single woman and single mother who manages without heroes".^[Corpus workbook, W219.]

The description is brief, and the text is not yet available to me in a form I can cite at length; I flag it, with the workbook, as the corpus's most important open item. But even the description changes the frame of this book. Every Sita examined so far, however radical, has been a queen. Kumaran Asan's thinks; Volga's learns; Kandasamy's desires and vanishes; Sengupta's leaves in a chariot. Each of them is a royal woman whose suffering is the suffering of a royal woman wronged. Sannathimmi's Sita is a single mother who manages: who works, who raises her children, who does not need Rama or Ravana or the sages or the gods. She is the Sita of the women who actually were abandoned with children and had to feed them. Dev Sen's songs gave glimpses of her (Sita giving birth alone, Sita with nothing to eat for five days);^[Nabaneeta Dev Sen, "When Women Retell the Ramayan," *Manushi* 108 (1998): 25.] Du. Saraswathi gives her a narrator who has lived it.

The corpus workbook's idea log asks of Sannathimmi and of Volga's Śabarī: "Do they change the plot, or only the speaker?"^[Corpus workbook, My Ideas tab, idea 7.] The question is exactly right, and on the evidence of the description, Sannathimmi changes the plot. A Sita who manages without heroes is a Sita for whom the heroic plot, the abduction and the rescue and the ordeal, is beside the point. The plot that matters is the plot of survival. It is the women's Rāmāyaṇa of Chapter 2, with the lament replaced by labour.

## Hindi, Marathi and the gap that remains

The corpus records one further lead that I have not been able to follow far enough to use: Rekha Sethi's 2026 study of seven contemporary Hindi women poets, including Dalit and Adivasi voices such as Sushila Takbhoure and Nirmala Putul, which the workbook identifies as "a map for finding Hindi women's Sita poems".^[Rekha Sethi, *An Alchemy of Words and Silences: Contemporary Hindi Women Poets* (2026) [W202].] The workbook also notes that Tara Bhavalkar's Marathi *Sītāyan*, listed by one bookseller, could not be confirmed.^[Corpus workbook, Read Me tab, "Corrections and checks".] I record these as directions for further work, and as an honest statement of this chapter's limits. The Dalit and Adivasi women's Sita has been written and sung. Much of it has not yet been gathered into the archive from which a book like this one is made.

## Conclusion

The feminist canon of Sita retellings has been, for most of its history, a canon about a queen. Its central drama, the rescue of Sita from the ordeal, is the drama of the upper-caste wife whose purity guards a lineage. It has attended less to the mutilated outsider, Śūrpaṇakhā, whose recent rehabilitation as a victim has often detached her from the caste and political logic of her mutilation; and it has attended least to Śabarī, whose touch was the question, and to the Dalit and Adivasi women for whom the epic's distribution of suffering was not a literary matter.

The anti-caste women writers have begun to change this. Meena Kandasamy's Sita climbs onto a stranger's lap and perfects the vanishing act, and her Śūrpaṇakhā speaks as a traitress to the boundary that maimed her. Hira Bansode asks Śabarī why she fed the god instead of questioning him. Du. Saraswathi's Sannathimmi tells of a Sita who manages without heroes. Their programmes are declared; their Sitas are theirs. And they bring into the story what the canon left out: desire, anger, labour, caste.

Part IV turns from the margins of the literary field to its new centre: the market. In the 2010s, the first-person Sita became a bestseller genre in English, the warrior Sita became a mass-market hero, and the screen made Sita, once again, a national property. The question this book has asked throughout, whose sentences Sita speaks, becomes in the market a question about who is buying them.
