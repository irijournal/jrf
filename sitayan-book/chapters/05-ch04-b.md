## Kuvempu: the husband in the fire

The third poet of the interior turn worked on a different scale. Kuppali Venkatappa Puttappa (1904–1994), who wrote as Kuvempu, was the most celebrated Kannada writer of the twentieth century, the first in his language to receive the Jnanpith Award, and a man born into the Vokkaliga peasantry of the Malnad who made an explicit programme of challenging the Brahmanical monopoly on learning. His Kannada play *Śūdra Tapasvi* (1944), in which Rama is brought to recognise the injustice of the Śambūka episode, is discussed in Chapter 5.^[Kuvempu, *Shudra Tapasvi* (1944) [W040]; excerpt in Paula Richman, ed., *Ramayana Stories in Modern South India: An Anthology* (Bloomington: Indiana University Press, 2008).] His masterpiece is the *Śrī Rāmāyaṇa Darśanaṃ*, a modern epic in blank verse in two volumes (1949 and 1957), which retells the whole story in the light of his philosophy of the "universal human" (*viśvamānava*), in which every character, Ravana and Mantharā included, is capable of spiritual growth and ultimately of redemption.^[Kuvempu, *Sri Ramayana Darshanam*, vol. 1 (1949), vol. 2 (1957) [W041].]

For the history of Sita, the decisive moment in Kuvempu's epic is the ordeal. The corpus record summarises it: "At the fire ordeal Rama rushes into the flames after Sita and walks out holding her hand."^[Corpus workbook, W041.] The image is unprecedented in the major tellings, and its significance should not be underestimated. In Vālmīki, Rama stands with his face turned down while Sita circumambulates him and enters the fire alone. In Kamban, the cosmos witnesses her purity. In Tulsī, the shadow vanishes and the substance returns. In none of them does Rama share the fire.

Kuvempu's Rama does. And in doing so he transforms the ordeal from a test that one party imposes on the other into an ordeal that both undergo. The fire tests the marriage, not the wife. The corpus workbook calls this "a canonical, award-winning epic that shares the ordeal between husband and wife instead of cancelling it".^[Corpus workbook, W041.] The distinction between sharing and cancelling is precise. The modern liberal response to the ordeal is to cancel it: to have Sita refuse it, or to have the author denounce it. Kuvempu does neither. He keeps the ordeal and redistributes its burden.

I find this solution admirable and also revealing. It is admirable because it is the first serious attempt in a major modern telling to imagine the ordeal as something other than a gendered proof; it asks what it would mean for the husband to submit to the same test he demands, and it answers by having him do so. It anticipates, by several decades, the analytic hypothesis drawn in Figure 1.3: that the *agni-parīkṣā* distributes scrutiny unequally, placing the burden of proof on the woman and the protection of reputation on the man. Kuvempu equalises the burden.

It is revealing because of what the equalisation leaves untouched. Rama enters the fire after Sita, not instead of her. The ordeal still takes place. The public still requires proof. The difference is that the king now shares in the proof. Kuvempu's universalism redeems Rama by making him a fellow sufferer, and in doing so it keeps intact the structure in which a woman's purity is a matter for public demonstration. This is the characteristic limit of the reforming humanism of the mid-twentieth century: it could imagine the husband joining the wife in the ordeal, but not the abolition of the ordeal. The interior turn, which gave Sita a mind, in Kuvempu also gave Rama a conscience, and the conscience took him into the fire. It did not take him to the conclusion that there should be no fire.

## The "after" as women's time

Before leaving the interior turn, I want to draw out a pattern that the research notes identified early and that this chapter confirms. In the corpus workbook's idea log, the first entry reads: "The 'after' as a women's genre: Chandrabati's lament, Kumaran Asan's reflecting Sita and Toru Dutt's mother all tell the story after the war. Is the time after the epic the women's part of it?"^[Corpus workbook, My Ideas tab, idea 1 (W015, W033, W028).]

The evidence assembled so far suggests that the answer is yes, and that the pattern is older and wider than the three texts named. The women singers whom Dev Sen recorded preferred the first and last books.^[Nabaneeta Dev Sen, "When Women Retell the Ramayan," *Manushi* 108 (1998): 19.] Bhavabhūti set his drama after the banishment. Vidyāsāgar retold the exile. Toru Dutt's mother narrates the second exile. Meher's *Tapasvinī* begins in the hermitage. Asan's Sita thinks on the evening her sons leave. In Chapter 7 we shall see that Mallika Sengupta's Bengali *Sitayan* (1996) begins on the day Lakshmana leaves Sita near the hermitage; in Chapter 11, that Pragya Agrawal's *The Renunciation* (2025) begins after the banishment and S. L. Bhyrappa's Kannada *Uttarakāṇḍa* (2017) has Sita look back.^[Mallika Sengupta, *Sitayan* (Kolkata: Ananda, 1996) [W214]; Pragya Agrawal, *The Renunciation* (New Delhi: Rupa, 2025) [W235]; S. L. Bhyrappa, *Uttarakaanda* (2017) [W223].]

Why should the time after the war be women's time? Three reasons suggest themselves, and they are not mutually exclusive.

The first is structural. The war is men's time: its actors are men, its values are the values Dev Sen listed when she described epic poets as "men singing the glory of other men—armed men, to be precise".^[Dev Sen, "When Women Retell," 18.] The time after the war is the time of consequences, and consequences are borne disproportionately by women. Sita's story after Laṅkā is a story of what the war, and the honour for which it was fought, cost her.

The second is generic. The time after is the time of memory, and memory is the natural domain of the lyric, the lament and the meditation. The *bāromāsī* that carries Candrābatī's narrative, the women's work songs, the long meditative poem: these are the forms of retrospection, and they are the forms in which women's literary traditions in India have been most richly developed.

The third is political. The time after is the time in which judgement becomes possible. During the war, there is no space to judge Rama, because he is fighting to recover Sita. After the war, after the ordeal, after the banishment, his conduct can be weighed. The women's time is the time of the verdict.

If this is right, then the interior turn did not simply invent Sita's inner life. It moved the modern Sita into the temporal territory that women's traditions had already claimed, and gave her there the means, through the long meditative poem, to deliver a verdict at the length and with the seriousness that the songs, in their brevity, could only gesture at.

**Position A:** Sita's silence is powerlessness: what she does not say is what she was not permitted to say.\
**Position B:** Sita's silence is strategic refusal: by withholding speech from a tribunal that would misread it, she preserves her judgement intact.\
**Where the evidence of this chapter leaves the argument.** The interior turn forces a distinction that the debate usually collapses: between silence of mind and silence of address. Asan's Sita is not silent in mind; she thinks at length and reaches a verdict. She is silent in address; she does not deliver the verdict to Rama. That silence is not powerlessness, because it is chosen and because it is followed by an act that delivers the verdict more decisively than speech could. But neither is it simply strategy, because the poem makes clear that no form of address was available that Rama's world would have heard as anything but insubordination. Position B is right about the act and Position A is right about the world.

## Conclusion

The interior turn of the early twentieth century gave the modern literature of Sita its first great gift: a Sita who thinks. Meher made her exile an ascetic discipline and her forest a community; Asan made her memory a tribunal and her return to the earth the execution of a reasoned verdict; Kuvempu made her ordeal a test that Rama shared. All three poets came from communities the caste order of their time held in contempt, and all three, in giving Sita an inner life, were claiming for her a form of spiritual and intellectual dignity that the order denied to them. The first modern Sitas who think were, in this sense, also anti-caste Sitas, and the liberation narrative that dates Sita's emancipation to the 1990s misses both facts.

But the interior turn also defined a limit. A Sita who thinks is not yet a Sita who is heard. The poets who followed, in the 1920s and after, set out to break that limit, by making Sita speak her judgement aloud, by turning the epic against itself, and by making Rama, rather than Sita, the defendant. They are the subject of Chapter 5. Their Sitas speak louder than any before them. The question is whose words they speak.
