# A Note on Evidence, Citation and Verification {.unnumbered}

This book is written to the standard set in its own research plan: four levels of evidence (primary literary texts; primary intellectual and historical sources; peer-reviewed scholarship; theoretical interlocutors), Chicago notes-and-bibliography referencing, and a refusal to invent dialogue, page numbers or passages, or to present a paraphrase as a quotation. Because the reader is entitled to know how far each citation has been checked, I state the conventions plainly.

1. **Classical texts are cited by canonical locator, not by page.** The Vālmīki *Rāmāyaṇa* is cited by book (*kāṇḍa*), canto (*sarga*) and verse in the Baroda critical edition, as transcribed in the Göttingen Register of Electronic Texts in Indian Languages (GRETIL). Every Sanskrit verse quoted in this book was checked against that text. Translations from Sanskrit are my own unless stated, and are deliberately literal. Where a reader is using the Princeton translation by Robert P. Goldman, Sally J. Sutherland Goldman and their collaborators, the verse numbers correspond, since that translation follows the critical edition. Kālidāsa is cited by canto and verse; the *Mahābhārata* by the critical edition's book and chapter; the *Rāmcaritmānas* by book.

2. **Pinpoint page numbers marked with no symbol have been verified** against the source in the course of writing. This applies, among others, to Nabaneeta Dev Sen's "When Women Retell the Ramayan" (*Manushi* 108, 1998, pp. 18–27) and Madhu Kishwar's "Yes to Sita, No to Ram!" (*Manushi* 98, 1997, pp. 20–31), both read in the journal's own scanned pages.

3. **A dagger (†) after a page number** marks a passage that is widely reproduced in the scholarly literature and cited here from my working knowledge of the standard edition, but which I was not able to re-check against a physical or scanned copy while writing. These must be checked before submission. They are few, and they are all theoretical texts (Rich, Beauvoir, Butler, Spivak, Mahmood, Kandiyoti).

4. **"(Pinpoint to be verified)"** marks a citation where the work and its argument are secure but where I do not have the page. In every such case I have refrained from direct quotation and given a paraphrase, clearly signalled as such. This is most often the case with the modern novels, which are in copyright and were not available to me in a citable edition during drafting. The reading notes for each novel are nonetheless based on the novels' structure, plot and narrative strategy as described in the corpus workbook, in publishers' descriptions and in published reviews, and they are written so that the page references can be inserted without altering the argument. Where a phrase from a novel is quoted, it is a phrase already recorded in my corpus workbook and is flagged for checking.

5. **Article page ranges** in the bibliography are given as published wherever they are known. A newspaper review or a magazine feature is never cited as though it were peer-reviewed scholarship; the notes say what kind of source each is.

6. **Corpus identifiers.** My research corpus, kept as a workbook titled *Sitayans: Feminist Retellings of the Ramayana*, holds 243 works, each with an identifier from W001 to W243. These identifiers appear in square brackets in the notes (for example [W015] for Candrābatī), so that every claim about a work can be traced back to the corpus record, its edition notes and its check notes. Where the workbook flags a date conflict or an unconfirmed detail, the text says so.

The figures fall into two kinds, and the captions say which is which. **Data figures** (the distribution of the corpus by period, language, form and character, and the decade counts) are computed from the workbook. **Interpretive figures** (the agency matrix, the voice-against-need plot, the spectrum of agency) encode my own readings. They are arguments drawn as pictures, and they should be argued with.

# A Note on Transliteration and Names {.unnumbered}

Sanskrit terms are transliterated in the International Alphabet of Sanskrit Transliteration (IAST) when they are discussed as terms (*agni-parīkṣā*, *pativratā*, *strīdharma*, *apavāda*). Names of epic characters are given with diacritics at first mention and in the figures (Sītā, Rāma, Lakṣmaṇa, Ūrmilā, Śūrpaṇakhā, Ahalyā, Kaikeyī, Mandodarī, Śabarī), and thereafter in the plain forms familiar to readers of Indian English (Sita, Rama, Lakshmana, Urmila, Surpanakha, Ahalya, Kaikeyi, Mandodari, Shabari). Titles of modern works are given as their authors or publishers give them, which means that the same name appears in different spellings (*Sitayan*, *Sitayana*, *Sitar Banabas*, *Seetha*). Hindi, Bengali, Telugu, Malayalam, Tamil, Kannada, Marathi, Odia and Assamese titles are transliterated simply, without diacritics, except where a diacritic changes the word.

I use "Bharat" and "India" interchangeably, as the Constitution does in its first Article. I use "Indic" for traditions of thought and practice that arose within the subcontinent, without prejudice to the many communities, Muslim and Christian among them, who have told the story of Rama and Sita in their own languages for centuries; the Persian, Malay, Mappila and Urdu tellings discussed in this book are part of that inheritance and are treated as such.

# Preface: Why Another Book on Sita? {.unnumbered}

Let me begin with a confession that will, I suspect, be shared by many readers of my generation. I did not first meet Sita in a book. I met her in the songs that the women of my family sang at weddings, in the calendar art that hung above the kitchen door, in the Sunday-morning television that my parents still describe as the only time the streets of their town were ever truly empty, and in the casual, devastating proverbs by which adults told girls what was expected of them. By the time I came to read the Vālmīki *Rāmāyaṇa*, first in translation and then, haltingly, in Sanskrit, I already "knew" Sita. What I knew was a composite: patient, chaste, silent, suffering, beautiful, a little dull. The first shock of reading Vālmīki was to discover that almost every one of those adjectives was wrong, and that the one which was most wrong was "silent".

This book grew out of that shock. It is a study of how modern Indian writers, across a dozen languages and a century and a half, have reconstructed Sita, and of what their reconstructions reveal about the societies that produced them. It is not a retelling, and it is not a celebration. Nor is it a prosecution. It is, if I may borrow the vocabulary of the profession I most admire for its discipline of argument, a brief: a sustained case, built on evidence, that answers a question and anticipates its strongest objections.

The question is this. When writers give Sita a voice, a body, a mind, an anger, a politics, are they recovering a woman who was always there and was silenced, or are they using her, as every age before them has used her, to say what their own age needs said? Put differently: is the literary afterlife of Sita a history of women's changing possibilities of speech, or a history of the changing political needs of the societies that keep rewriting her?

My answer, which I shall defend at length, is that it is both, but not in equal measure, and not in the way that the dominant accounts suppose. The dominant account, which I shall call the *liberation narrative*, holds that the "traditional" Sita was a silent, submissive model of wifely devotion constructed by Brahmanical patriarchy, and that modern feminist writers have progressively liberated her by giving her a voice. I shall argue that this narrative is wrong at its beginning, because the Sita of Vālmīki is anything but silent; wrong in its middle, because the silent and submissive Sita is in large part a product of the nineteenth-century colonial encounter and the nationalist response to it, not of "tradition" as such; and wrong at its end, because many modern "liberated" Sitas are liberated into a grammar of individual self-assertion that the tradition did not need and that serves the political needs of the moment as surely as the ideal wife served the needs of the reformer and the nationalist. What remains constant across the most powerful tellings, ancient and modern, is not voice but refusal: the refusal of a second ordeal, the refusal to return, the refusal to be judged by a tribunal that has already decided.

I am conscious that this argument will be read, in some quarters, as a defence of tradition against feminism, and in others as an attack on tradition dressed up in decolonial vocabulary. It is neither. My debt to feminist scholarship, and in particular to the historians and literary critics who taught a generation of Indian students to ask who speaks and who is spoken for, is immense and will be evident on almost every page. Uma Chakravarti's account of Brahmanical patriarchy, Kumkum Sangari and Sudesh Vaid's recasting of colonial history, Susie Tharu and K. Lalita's recovery of women's writing, Sharmila Rege's insistence on the Dalit feminist standpoint, Chandra Talpade Mohanty's critique of the universal "Third World Woman" and Gayatri Chakravorty Spivak's question about who can speak are, all of them, indispensable. But indispensable is not the same as unanswerable. Where I disagree, I say so and give reasons. I hope I have extended to these scholars the courtesy they extended to the texts they read: close attention, followed by argument.

I am equally conscious that I write as a student of history preparing for the profession, not as a senior scholar, and that a book of this scope invites error. The notes have been built to make error visible and correctable. Every work in the corpus carries an identifier that links it to the research workbook on which the book rests; every citation that I could not verify against the source while writing is marked as such; and the reader who wishes to check a claim about Vālmīki can do so verse by verse. If the book has a methodological virtue, it is that it does not hide what it does not know.

The structure is simple. An Introduction sets out the argument and the method. Part I examines the inheritance: Vālmīki's Sita, the regional, devotional and women's traditions, and the colonial production of the ideal wife. Part II follows the rebels and reformers of the twentieth century: the poets who first gave Sita an interior life, the rationalists and anti-caste thinkers who turned the epic against itself, and the writers who moved from Sita to the women at her edges. Part III takes up feminist reconstruction proper, from Candrābatī's sixteenth-century Bengali poem and Nabaneeta Dev Sen's recovery of it, through Volga, Sarah Joseph and Ambai, to the Dalit and anti-caste poets who ask whose Sita is being universalised. Part IV examines the market, the screen and the nation: the first-person Sita of the bestseller, the warrior Sita of the popular novel and the 2026 film, the disappearing Sita of Samhita Arni and Nina Paley, and the Hindi poet Anamika's claim to a feminism rooted in the soil rather than imported. A Conclusion returns to the earth that opens at the end of Vālmīki's poem and asks what it means that the most enduring image of Sita's agency is an act of withdrawal.

Each chapter contains, in addition to the argument, two kinds of box. The first, titled **Reading Against the Text**, poses a provocative interpretive question that the chapter does not fully answer, so that the reader may carry it into her own reading. The second, titled **The Debate**, sets out competing positions on one of the questions that run through the book (agency, chastity, marriage, exile, motherhood, silence, feminism, tradition) and states where I stand. The figures, of which there are many, are meant to do intellectual work, not to decorate. Where a figure is an argument, its caption says so.

Finally, a word on tone. I have written in the first person, and I have written to persuade. Scholarship that pretends to have no position usually has one and conceals it. I would rather state mine and let the reader judge whether the evidence supports it.
